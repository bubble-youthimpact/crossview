South Africa’s Anti-Immigrant Violence Strains Ties With African Neighbours

4 min read·Youth Correspondents

In April 2026, a group of men broke down the door of a shop in Durban owned by a Cameroonian national who had lived in South Africa for nearly twenty years. The men were affiliated with a movement called March and March, which had organised demonstrations against foreign nationals in Pretoria, Johannesburg, and Durban in the preceding weeks. Some of those demonstrations turned fatal. Human rights observers reported that police largely did not intervene.

The Durban incident is the latest in a series of attacks on foreign nationals that researchers and rights groups say has been building for three decades, with little substantive policy response from successive governments.

The Economic Context

South Africa has one of the highest unemployment rates in the world. As of early 2025, the official rate stood above 30%. When including people who have stopped looking for work, the figure rises to 43.7%, according to South Africa’s Quarterly Labour Force Survey (Q1 2025). Among young Black South Africans, unemployment exceeds 45%. The labour force participation rate recently fell to its lowest point since 2022.

Researchers who study xenophobic violence say these conditions are a significant factor in explaining — though not justifying — attacks on foreign nationals. Migrants who operate small businesses in township economies are often visible targets, and some South Africans blame them for limited job opportunities.

Economists and migration scholars, however, broadly dispute this framing. South Africa’s approximately 2.2 million African migrants represent around 3.5% of the population, and most analyses attribute the unemployment crisis to structural factors: deteriorating infrastructure, a persistently underperforming education system, and governance failures across multiple administrations. Whether migration has a meaningful impact on employment remains a contested empirical question, though the dominant view among researchers is that it does not explain the scale of joblessness.

A Recurring Pattern

The first major wave of coordinated xenophobic violence occurred in May 2008, when attacks broke out in at least 135 locations across the country. More than 60 people were killed and thousands were displaced, according to the African Diaspora Forum. The victims were predominantly Black African migrants — Zimbabweans, Mozambicans, and Somalis.

Between 1994 and August 2025, xenophobic attacks resulted in 694 deaths, 5,648 looted shops, and nearly 129,000 displacements, according to Xenowatch, an African xenophobia monitoring project based at the University of the Witwatersrand. Further major incidents occurred in 2015, killing seven people in Durban and Johannesburg, and in 2019, when fresh waves of violence prompted several African countries to recall their ambassadors to Pretoria.

Analysts who track political violence in South Africa note that the frequency and organisation of anti-migrant movements appear to have increased since roughly 2013, though they disagree on the precise causes.

Shifting Political Norms

Political observers point to a shift in the willingness of public figures to use anti-immigrant rhetoric openly. During Nelson Mandela’s presidency, explicit campaigns targeting foreign nationals were rare among mainstream politicians. That has changed in subsequent administrations, critics argue, with some politicians drawing links between migration and unemployment in ways that researchers say are not supported by evidence.

The administrations of Thabo Mbeki, Jacob Zuma, and Cyril Ramaphosa each faced criticism from rights groups for failing to prosecute xenophobic attacks as hate crimes or to implement sustained economic policies that might address underlying conditions. Under Zuma, widespread state capture — extensively documented by the subsequent Zondo Commission — further weakened institutions.

In this environment, anti-immigrant organisations have grown more prominent. Operation Dudula conducted raids on migrant-owned businesses and, in early 2024, physically blocked Zimbabweans at the Beitbridge border crossing. In November 2025, a court ordered the group to stop preventing migrants from accessing healthcare. March and March, the organisation behind the 2026 attacks, emerged more recently but follows a similar pattern, according to migrant rights groups.

“These organisations feed off the country’s frustrations and socioeconomic rights regression,” said one South African migrant rights activist, who asked not to be named for safety reasons.

A Historical Dimension

Several analysts have noted a historical dimension to the crisis. During South Africa’s anti-apartheid struggle, many of the countries whose nationals are now targets of violence — including Nigeria, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Tanzania, and Zambia — provided financial support, sanctuary, or military training to the African National Congress. The frontline states bore significant economic and security costs in support of South Africa’s liberation movement.

Whether this history creates particular obligations on the part of the South African government is a matter of ongoing debate, but the irony is frequently cited by African Union officials and regional diplomats when discussing the current violence.

What Experts and Advocates Propose

Human Rights Watch, in a May 2026 report, called on the South African government to prosecute anti-immigrant violence under existing hate crimes legislation, strengthen community policing in areas with high migrant populations, and increase investment in township economies.

Regional observers have also argued that diplomatic pressure from neighbouring states and the African Union could play a more sustained role. The ambassador recalls of 2019 had a short-term effect on the political response in Pretoria, but consistent multilateral pressure has not been maintained since, according to analysts at the Institute for Security Studies.

South African government officials have periodically condemned xenophobic violence in public statements, while also facing criticism from rights organisations for what they describe as a gap between those statements and enforcement on the ground.
