Not All Migrants Are Equal: South Africa's Unspoken Hierarchy

3 min read·Youth Correspondents

South Africa's xenophobic violence has a consistent target profile—and it is not random. The attacks have concentrated overwhelmingly on migrants from neighboring African countries, while nationals from Europe and the broader Global North living in the same cities have faced no comparable hostility. That pattern runs through three decades of documented incidents and has rarely been examined as directly as the violence itself.

Who Gets Attacked and Who Does Not

The demonstrations, raids, and killings that have marked South Africa's anti-immigrant crisis are directed at Zimbabweans, Mozambicans, Somalis, and Cameroonians—not at British, German, or French nationals who also live and work in South Africa in significant numbers. Polish, Romanian, and Bulgarian migrants, groups that occupy their own lower rungs in Europe's informal migration hierarchies, face no equivalent targeting in South African townships either.

Within the group of African migrants, further distinctions apply. Nigerians and other West Africans face stereotypes surrounding crime and drug trafficking that attach less readily to Zimbabwean or Mozambican migrants, who tend to be framed primarily in terms of economic competition. The hostility is not uniform. It is tiered, and those tiers broadly track migrants' origins and perceived social status.

A CNN report from June 2026 captured the broader dynamic: migrants from neighboring countries face the greatest risk of xenophobic attacks yet receive little international protection, while those who retained significant economic advantages after apartheid are shielded by private security firms and fortified suburban infrastructure. Thirty years after apartheid formally ended, the economic geography of South Africa still largely reflects that era's divisions. The violence erupting in townships does not challenge that geography. It operates alongside it.

The Afrikaner Refugee Question

The hierarchy became an international issue in February 2025, when the Trump administration launched a refugee program known as Mission South Africa, offering asylum to Afrikaner South Africans on the grounds of alleged systemic persecution.

The numbers that followed were striking. All but three of the 4,499 refugees admitted to the United States in fiscal year 2026 came from South Africa under this program, according to the Christian Science Monitor. The overall U.S. refugee admissions cap was set at a record low of 7,500 for the year, compared with more than 100,000 during the last full year of the Biden administration.

South African President Cyril Ramaphosa, speaking at the White House in May 2025, rejected the premise: "There is criminality in our country. People who do get killed, unfortunately, through criminal activity are not only white people. The majority of them are Black people," PBS reported. Nechama Brodie, a journalist who wrote a book on farm murders in South Africa, said that the Afrikaner community has not faced systemic persecution, either in the past or the present.

The Episcopal Church, a longtime U.S. refugee services provider, announced that it would end its partnership with the government rather than participate in the program, citing its commitment to racial justice. Researchers at Harvard's Carr-Ryan Center for Human Rights noted that the speed of processing demonstrated how effectively refugee infrastructure can be mobilized—and raised questions about the criteria by which that mobilization is triggered.

What the Pattern Reflects

The Helen Suzman Foundation, in an analysis of South African migration policy, concluded that as race was formally removed from political discourse after apartheid, hostility toward migrants moved in to fill the space, giving the state's erosion of migrants' rights a new legitimacy that no longer needed to name what it was doing.

Migrants from the countries that sheltered ANC members during the liberation struggle, provided military training, and bore real economic costs in support of South Africa's transition—Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Tanzania, and Zambia—are now among those most frequently targeted by anti-immigrant groups. Meanwhile, a foreign government classified a separate group of South Africans as refugees in need of priority protection on the grounds of a persecution that South Africa's own president, along with most independent researchers, says does not meet that threshold.

Many migrants from neighboring countries arrived in South Africa expecting a degree of welcome, based on the solidarity the continent had extended to South Africans during apartheid. According to research published in Frontiers in Political Science in January 2026, that expectation was historically grounded. What they encountered instead was a hierarchy that no policy document ever formally established—and that functions, in practice, with considerable consistency.

Sources: CNN (June 2026); Christian Science Monitor (April 2026); PBS NewsHour (May 2026); Harvard Carr-Ryan Center for Human Rights; Helen Suzman Foundation; Frontiers in Political Science (January 2026); Human Sciences Research Council South African Social Attitudes Survey (2025).
