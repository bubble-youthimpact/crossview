What Does It Mean to Leave the Happiest Country on Earth?

4 min read·Youth Correspondents

For decades, Bhutan has been held up as a counterexample to how countries measure progress. Where most governments track GDP, Bhutan tracks something else. The concept of Gross National Happiness—first articulated by the Fourth King, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, in the early 1970s with the declaration that "Gross National Happiness is more important than Gross Domestic Product"—became the country's official development philosophy, measuring progress through cultural preservation, environmental sustainability, and psychological well-being rather than economic output, according to the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative.

The philosophy earned Bhutan genuine international admiration. It also coexists, somewhat uncomfortably, with a more recent trend: its young people are leaving in numbers the country has never seen before.

The Exodus

Monthly departures from Paro International Airport—Bhutan's only international gateway—rose from fewer than 500 before the COVID-19 pandemic to more than 5,000 by early 2023, according to World Bank exit data. Migration doubled between 2020 and 2024, with the majority of those leaving being young and educated—53 percent held university degrees, and 49 percent were civil servants, the World Bank reported. Australia has become the primary destination. Between August 2023 and October 2024, Bhutan received $210 million in remittances, of which $132 million came from Australia alone, according to the Royal Monetary Authority.

The World Bank's acting country director for Bhutan, Cecile Fruman, called it "a classic case of brain drain," adding, "These are truly the most skilled people who are leaving the country, and this directly impacts the economy," Newsreel Asia reported.

The sectors hit hardest are those a country can least afford to lose. In March 2025, Bhutan's Ministry of Education announced plans to rehire retired or resigned teachers to fill 1,126 vacancies nationwide, with some schools having gone months without subject teachers, Newsreel Asia reported. The healthcare system faces a shortage of 172 doctors and 824 nurses—gaps significant enough that the Ministry of Health told Parliament in June 2024 that it might need to hire foreign medical professionals.

Why Leave the Happiest Country?

The reasons are less paradoxical than they appear. Bhutan's former Finance Minister, Namgay Tshering, has noted that Gross National Happiness was never about individual happiness—it is about collective well-being, sustainability, and inclusivity, according to Chulalongkorn University. A philosophy built around communal and spiritual flourishing does not automatically translate into the kind of economic opportunities that educated young professionals are looking for.

Bhutan's national unemployment rate doubled from 3 percent before the pandemic to 6 percent in 2022, with urban women and young people aged 20 to 24 particularly affected, The Star reported. More than 60 percent of the workforce reported being overworked, and many lacked formal contracts. In 2024 alone, more than 2,000 civil servants resigned—70 percent of them voluntarily.

Many of those leaving are not desperate. As Asia News Network reported, they are "the very cohort the country can least afford to lose: educated, experienced, and ambitious"—driven by disillusionment, not destitution.

The Role of Social Media

In 1999, during the celebration of the Fourth King's Silver Jubilee, Bhutan became the last country on Earth to legalize television and the internet, according to the University of Pennsylvania's Fels Institute of Government. Cell phones followed in 2003. The government's caution was deliberate—authorities banned MTV and professional wrestling broadcasts on the grounds of their potential effect on young people, the Los Angeles Times reported at the time.

What happened next moved faster than the government anticipated. Internet subscribers rose from 5,726 in 2008 to 251,441 in 2013, driven largely by Facebook's rapid adoption, according to research published in the International Journal of Communication. The country, described by researchers as a traditionally oral culture, leapfrogged decades of media evolution—bypassing print and moving directly to digital platforms, according to a study published in the Journal of Bhutan Studies. In a country where more than 60 percent of the population is under the age of 22, according to BBC reporting, social media did more than connect young Bhutanese to one another. It connected them to the outside world.

Young Bhutanese can now see, in real time, what their peers in Australia or Canada earn, how they live, and how accessible the visa process has become. The emigration surge that followed the pandemic is difficult to separate from that visibility. As Bhutan's former Finance Minister acknowledged, young people in the digital age face pressures and comparisons that no government philosophy fully anticipated, Chulalongkorn University reported.

What the Government is Doing

The Bhutanese government has responded with a mandatory program called Gyalsung, requiring all citizens turning 18 to undergo a year of training in construction, computing, entrepreneurship, and agriculture. The program is intended to upskill young people and reduce the mismatch between education and available jobs, according to the Observer Research Foundation. The prime minister has been direct about the stakes, stating that "we do not want a Bhutan without Bhutanese."

The program is running. Whether it is working is less clear. The World Bank has warned that the more Bhutan's most educated young people prefer to live and work abroad, the harder it will be to translate decades of educational investment into national development, and that the concentration of young people among migrants is already accelerating the aging of Bhutanese society.

Bhutan built its reputation on the proposition that a country could choose a different kind of progress—slower, deeper, and less legible to the outside world. That proposition has not been abandoned. But it is now being weighed, by the people it was designed to serve, against the prospect of a better salary and a faster internet connection somewhere else. So far, a growing number of them are choosing the latter.
