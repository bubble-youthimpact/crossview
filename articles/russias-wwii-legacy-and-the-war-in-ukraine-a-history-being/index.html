Russia’s WWII Legacy and the War in Ukraine: A History Being Fought Over

3 min read·Youth Correspondents

The Soviet Union’s role in defeating Nazi Germany is, by most historians’ accounts, unambiguous in scale. The Soviet Union lost approximately 27 million people during the war — more than any other nation — including an estimated 8.7 million military deaths, according to figures cited by EBSCO Research and the Russian Academy of Sciences. By comparison, Germany suffered around 5.3 million military casualties, the majority of them on the Eastern Front.

More recent historiography has reinforced this picture. According to a review published in the Journal of Slavic Military Studies, the fundamental battles of the Second World War in Europe were fought between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, and it was on the Eastern Front that the Wehrmacht suffered most of its casualties and where the outcome of the war in Europe was effectively decided. By one estimate, German military deaths on the Eastern Front numbered 2.7 million by the end of 1944, compared to 340,000 on the Western Front.

During the Cold War, however, Western accounts of the war tended to centre on the Allied landings in Normandy as the decisive turning point, according to the same journal. Historians have since argued that this framing left the Soviet contribution systematically underweighted in popular Western memory — a gap that persists in public consciousness despite the scholarly record.

Russia’s “Denazification” Claim and the Problem With It

Since launching its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Russia has framed the war as a “special military operation” aimed at the “denazification” of Ukraine. The claim draws directly on WWII symbolism — positioning Russia as the inheritor of the Soviet anti-fascist legacy and Ukraine’s government as its ideological enemy.

The argument has a fundamental problem: Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelensky, is Jewish. His family lost relatives in the Holocaust. He has attended Holocaust commemorations in Ukraine and visited Israel in an official capacity.

Russia’s response to this contradiction has drawn widespread condemnation. In a televised interview in September 2023, Putin alleged, without providing evidence, that Western powers had installed Zelensky as president specifically to “cover up” the glorification of Nazism, the Jerusalem Post reported. He further claimed that his “Jewish friends” considered Zelensky “a disgrace to the Jewish people” — a statement the Jewish Confederation of Ukraine publicly rejected.

Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov went further. When asked in May 2022 how Ukraine could be called a Nazi country when its president is Jewish, Lavrov suggested that Hitler himself had Jewish origins, according to Fox News. Israel’s foreign minister condemned the remarks as “unforgivable” and “a terrible historical error.”

Zelensky, for his part, has repeatedly described Russian military conduct as “pure Nazi behaviour,” appealing to Jewish communities abroad for support against the invasion, Reuters reported.

The Historical Irony

What makes the “denazification” framing particularly striking to historians is that it inverts a verifiable historical record. The Soviet Union — and Russia as its largest successor state — did bear the greatest human cost in defeating Nazi Germany. That history is real and documented.

But deploying that legacy to justify an invasion, and using antisemitic tropes against a Jewish head of state in the process, has led analysts and governments across Europe to argue that Russia is not honouring its WWII history so much as weaponising it. The country that suffered most from Nazi racial ideology is, in the view of its critics, now using the language of that same era to target a democratic neighbour led by a Jewish president whose family lived through the Holocaust.

Whether that constitutes a distortion of history, a deliberate propaganda strategy, or both, is a matter of ongoing debate. What is not in dispute is the underlying record: the Soviet Union’s contribution to defeating Nazism was decisive, and the people now being called Nazis by Moscow include the democratically elected Jewish president of a country where millions of Jews were killed by actual Nazi forces during the Second World War.
