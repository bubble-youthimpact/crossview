Power Shift in the Sahel: Coups, Resources, and the Retreat of France

4 min read·Youth Correspondents

In January 2022, Burkina Faso’s military ousted its elected president. Eight months later, the junta that replaced him was itself overthrown. Captain Ibrahim Traoré, then 34, emerged as the country’s new leader — the second military figure to seize power in a single calendar year, according to the Africa Center for Strategic Studies.

Burkina Faso is not an outlier. Mali had two coups between 2020 and 2021. Guinea fell to a coup in September 2021. Niger followed in July 2023, when General Abdourahamane Tchiani seized power and placed the elected president under house arrest. The stretch of West and Central Africa running through these countries — the Sahel — has become the world’s most active zone of military takeovers.

Anti-French Sentiment and Who Fills the Gap

Each coup has come with a common political refrain: France must go. In January 2023, Burkina Faso’s junta terminated its military accord with France and ordered French troops out within a month. Niger followed the same script after its 2023 coup — crowds stormed the French embassy in Niamey, and the military government expelled French forces and diplomatic staff, the Christian Science Monitor reported.

The grievances are not entirely without basis. France has maintained a military presence across its former Sahelian colonies for decades, framing it as counterterrorism support. Critics, including the juntas themselves, have argued that decades of French involvement produced neither stability nor meaningful economic development. When Macron told French ambassadors in January 2025 that no Sahelian country would be sovereign today without the French military — and suggested the region had forgotten to say thank you — the statement landed in West Africa as confirmation of exactly the attitude the juntas had spent years citing as their justification, Al Jazeera reported.

Into the space left by France’s departure, Russia has moved. Wagner Group, the Russian private military company, had been operating in Mali since 2021. After its founder Yevgeny Prigozhin died in a plane crash in 2023, it was rebranded as Africa Corps, but operations continued. In Burkina Faso, Africa Corps arrived in late 2022 in a security-for-resources arrangement — military training and protection in exchange for access to gold mines, Modern Diplomacy reported. Traoré visited Moscow for Russia’s WWII Victory Parade in 2025, cementing what analysts describe as the region’s clearest example of the model.

Uranium: The Resource at the Centre of It All

Niger’s strategic importance is difficult to separate from what lies underground. The country holds one of the world’s largest uranium reserves, and for over fifty years France’s state-linked energy company Orano operated mines there. Niger supplied roughly 20 percent of France’s uranium imports over the past decade, the Foreign Policy Research Institute reported — a meaningful share for a country that generates approximately 70 percent of its electricity from nuclear power.

After the 2023 coup, Niger’s junta accused France of decades of resource extraction that left the population with little to show for it. One mine closed in 2021 reportedly left behind 20 million tons of radioactive waste near the northern town of Arlit. In June 2025, the junta nationalized SOMAIR, Orano’s main subsidiary, seizing roughly 1,150 metric tons of stockpiled uranium and ending half a century of French control over the industry, Africa Eye reported.

In late 2025, a heavily armed convoy of trucks carrying the uranium left the Arlit mine site — dubbed the “Mad Max convoy” by French media, and cited by Foreign Policy as a symbol of an international order being rapidly rewired. The convoy reached Niamey’s military airport, where it has since stalled. The planned route through Benin collapsed after a failed coup attempt there in December 2025 closed that corridor. A Russian cargo vessel waiting off the coast of Togo left without its shipment. As of early 2026, the uranium remains at a military base in Niger, frozen by an international court order and contested by France, Russia, and the United States — each with competing claims over what happens to it next.

Instability, Displacement, and Europe’s Concern

The security vacuum left by departing Western forces has not been filled cleanly. Jihadist insurgencies linked to al-Qaeda and the Islamic State continue to expand. In 2022, Burkina Faso and Mali recorded their highest levels of conflict fatalities since the Sahel crisis began — 4,266 and 4,867 respectively, according to ACLED data cited by the Mixed Migration Centre. Over 11,100 schools across the Sahel were closed due to conflict or direct threats against teachers, UNICEF reported.

The consequences move. Displacement begins within countries, spreads across West Africa, and eventually reaches North Africa and the Mediterranean. The EU, which has invested billions in migration management through its Emergency Trust Fund for Africa, has acknowledged that Sahelian instability directly drives irregular migration toward Europe. Brussels expressed particular alarm after Niger’s 2023 coup — Niger had served as a key transit point for migrants moving north, and its collapse as a cooperative partner removed one of the main checkpoints on that route, GIS Reports noted.

Five years ago, France had troops in all three countries, uranium flowing from its mines in Niger, and governments in Ouagadougou, Bamako, and Niamey that were, at minimum, willing to engage with Paris. Today, French forces are gone, Orano has been expelled, and over 1,000 tonnes of uranium sit in legal limbo at a Sahelian military base. How the region looks in another five years is a question no one in Paris, Moscow, or Washington can yet answer with confidence.
