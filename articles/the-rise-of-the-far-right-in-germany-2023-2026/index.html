The Rise of the Far Right in Germany, 2023–2026

4 min read·Youth Correspondents

Something has been shifting in German politics over the past few years. It hasn’t happened all at once, and it doesn’t look the same everywhere, but the direction is hard to miss. The far right is no longer sitting at the edges. It is closer to the center now.

At the core of this shift is Alternative for Germany. When the party first appeared in 2013, it focused mostly on the European Union and economic policy, but that phase didn’t last long, and over time its messaging turned toward immigration, national identity, and cultural anxiety in ways that pulled in a different kind of support. By 2024, it was polling around one-fifth of the national vote, and in eastern states like Thuringia and Saxony, where economic frustration and political distrust run deeper, it climbed much higher, sometimes past thirty percent in regional elections. That is not marginal support anymore.

The state has taken notice, and not quietly. The Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution moved from calling the party “suspected extremist” to labeling it outright extremist in 2025 after years of investigation, a step that allows closer monitoring and signals a clear level of concern from within the system itself. That designation did not come out of nowhere. It followed a pattern.

Some of that pattern shows up in the party’s leadership. Björn Höcke, one of its most visible figures, has been fined for using a banned Nazi-era slogan and has repeatedly made statements about Germany’s memory culture that have drawn sharp criticism, including calling the Holocaust memorial in Berlin a “monument of shame,” while a court later confirmed that describing him as a fascist falls within legal bounds. Words matter here. They travel.

Then there was Potsdam. In late 2023, a meeting took place behind closed doors, bringing together AfD members, business figures, and far-right activists like Martin Sellner, and when journalists revealed the details months later, the reaction was immediate and loud. The discussion had centered on “remigration,” a term that sounds technical but, in this context, pointed toward large-scale deportations, even including people with German citizenship under certain conditions. That idea landed heavily. People went to the streets.

Protests followed across the country, week after week in some places, with crowds that cut across age groups and regions, and for a moment the political mood felt unsettled, almost raw, as if something that had been building quietly had suddenly been pulled into the open. This was not a small reaction.

Still, the electoral trend did not reverse. In the 2025 federal elections, the AfD finished second, which placed it firmly inside the national political conversation whether other parties liked it or not, and even though it stayed out of government, its presence began to shape decisions made elsewhere. Lines started to blur. That matters.

One of those shifts came when parts of the CDU/CSU worked alongside the AfD on a migration-related vote, something that would have been difficult to imagine not long ago, and at the same time Chancellor Friedrich Merz moved toward stricter migration policies, tightening borders and limiting family reunification in ways that echoed concerns the AfD had been raising for years. The overlap was noticeable. People noticed.

Beyond parliament, the atmosphere has changed as well. Reports from 2024 showed a sharp rise in politically motivated crimes, with a large share linked to right-wing extremism, and increases in antisemitic and anti-Muslim incidents were recorded at the same time, suggesting that political language and social behavior were moving together rather than apart. The tone of public life has shifted. It feels sharper.

Why this is happening is harder to pin down neatly, and simple answers don’t hold up for long, but certain threads come up again and again, including concerns about immigration after 2015, uneven economic conditions between regions, and a sense among some voters that mainstream parties no longer speak to them, especially younger people who are now being reached through social media channels that reward strong, direct messaging. It spreads fast. Faster than before.

And yet, the story does not move in one direction only. The protests, the legal scrutiny, the continued refusal by most parties to form coalitions with the AfD—these all point to a system that is still pushing back, even if unevenly, and still trying to hold a line that was drawn after the country’s past experiences with extremism. That line is under pressure.

Looking toward the next round of state elections, especially in the east, the question is no longer whether the far right can win votes. It already has. The question is what happens if it turns those votes into actual governing power, and how the rest of the political system responds when that moment comes. That is where things may shift again.
