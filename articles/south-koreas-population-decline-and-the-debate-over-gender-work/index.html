South Korea’s Population Decline and the Debate Over Gender, Work, and Family

3 min read·Youth Correspondents

South Korea is facing one of the world’s most severe population declines. In 2023, the country recorded a total fertility rate of 0.72 children per woman, the lowest ever recorded globally at the national level according to data from Statistics Korea. Similar trends are appearing in countries such as Japan, Italy, and China, where aging populations, delayed marriage, and declining birth rates are reshaping economies and social systems. Yet the public conversation surrounding these demographic shifts is often deeply gendered, particularly in discussions about women, career advancement, and family life.

A common argument in conservative media and online discourse is that women are choosing careers over marriage and motherhood. Some demographic data does show that younger women increasingly prioritize higher education, financial independence, and professional stability before considering marriage or children. In South Korea, the average age of first marriage and childbirth has steadily risen over the past two decades, while labor force participation among highly educated women has increased (OECD, 2024). However, many feminist scholars and social analysts argue that reducing the issue to “women no longer wanting children” ignores broader structural realities and places disproportionate responsibility on women alone.

One major criticism is that public discourse rarely asks the same questions of men. Women are often scrutinized for delaying marriage, focusing on careers, or rejecting traditional family roles, while male participation in childcare, emotional labor, and domestic responsibilities receives comparatively less attention. South Korea continues to have one of the largest gender wage gaps among OECD countries, and women still carry a disproportionate burden of unpaid household and caregiving work (OECD Gender Data Portal, 2024). Feminist perspectives argue that fertility decline cannot be understood separately from gender inequality.

Government and workplace conditions also play a major role in reproductive decisions. Surveys conducted by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs have repeatedly shown that economic insecurity, housing costs, unstable employment, and the pressure of raising children in an intensely competitive education system discourage young adults from starting families. Women in particular report fears about career interruption after childbirth, workplace discrimination, and inadequate institutional support for balancing work and family life.

South Korea has introduced financial incentives, childcare subsidies, and parental leave policies in an attempt to reverse demographic decline. Yet critics argue that financial measures alone cannot solve the problem if workplace culture remains rigid and caregiving expectations remain unequal. Although paternity leave formally exists, men often face workplace stigma for using it, while women continue to experience career penalties associated with motherhood. According to OECD Family Database data, South Korea’s working hours remain among the highest in the developed world, further complicating family formation.

The debate has become increasingly polarized online. Some commentators frame low birth rates as evidence of feminism weakening traditional family structures while feminist writers argue that the crisis reflects systemic failures rather than individual selfishness. Similar debates are now emerging in China and Japan, where governments encourage childbirth while women increasingly criticize unequal social expectations and insufficient support systems.

Ultimately, South Korea’s population decline is not simply a story about women rejecting motherhood. It is tied to economic pressure, housing insecurity, workplace culture, gender inequality, and changing social values. The issue persists because modern societies increasingly expect women to succeed professionally while still carrying much of the traditional burden of caregiving. Without addressing those structural conditions, many analysts argue that declining birth rates are unlikely to reverse significantly.
