Dividing the Water: Central Asia's Most Dangerous Resource Dispute

5 min read·Youth Correspondents

The Soviet Union solved Central Asia's water problem by decree. Upstream republics—Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, situated in the mountains where the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers originate—would release water downstream during the summer irrigation season. In exchange, downstream republics—Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, and Turkmenistan—would ship oil and gas back upstream in winter, when energy demand peaked. The system was inefficient and ecologically destructive, but it held together because Moscow could compel it to, according to the OECD.

When the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, so did the arrangement. What had been an internal Soviet logistical problem became a dispute among five newly independent sovereign states, each asserting control over the same rivers for incompatible purposes.

The Basic Conflict

The fault line runs along a simple geographic divide. Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan are water-rich but energy-poor. The rivers flowing through their mountains generate enormous hydroelectric potential, but the two countries have little oil or gas. Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, and Turkmenistan lie downstream, where those same rivers irrigate vast cotton fields that remain central to their agricultural economies, according to the OECD.

Under the Soviet system, upstream states released water in summer for irrigation and received fossil fuels in return for winter heating. That arrangement broke down in the early 2000s, when Uzbek President Islam Karimov began cutting discounted winter energy supplies to his upstream neighbors, the Christian Science Monitor reported. Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, facing energy crises, began releasing more water in winter to generate electricity, precisely when downstream farmers did not need it, and revived Soviet-era plans for massive new dams.

The Dam Question

Tajikistan's Rogun Dam on the Vakhsh River has been the central flashpoint. Construction began in 1976 under the Soviets, was repeatedly delayed by floods and civil war, and resumed in 2016. Once completed, it will be the world's tallest dam, producing 3.6 gigawatts of electricity, according to the IEA Energy Sector Review. For Tajikistan, which relies on hydropower for more than 90 percent of its electricity and has long faced severe winter shortages, the dam is an energy security issue with no obvious alternative.

For downstream Uzbekistan, the picture looked different. The dam would alter the seasonal flow of the Amu Darya, the primary source of water for Uzbek cotton fields that accounted for around 13 percent of the country's total export value as of 2019, according to the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace. Former President Islam Karimov did not use diplomatic language when discussing it. Speaking in Astana in 2012, he warned that water disputes could "spark not simply serious confrontation but even wars," Eurasianet reported.

Kyrgyzstan is pursuing a parallel project, the Kambarata-1 Dam on the Naryn River, which feeds the Syr Darya through Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan toward the Aral Sea. The World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, the Islamic Development Bank, and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development have all expressed interest in financing it, according to the Atlantic Council.

When the Argument Turned Violent

The dispute over dams and water-sharing agreements had a more immediate, local dimension: roughly 40 shared water channels run through the Kyrgyz-Tajik border zone, where farmers from both sides compete for irrigation water every spring, according to the Jamestown Foundation. Between 1991 and 2022, at least 150 clashes over disputed territory occurred between the two countries, most of them at the local level.

In April 2021, a dispute over control of a waterworks facility near the Tajik exclave of Vorukh escalated into an interstate military conflict. At least 55 people were killed, and more than 40,000 civilians were displaced, according to Human Rights Watch. The following September, a larger clash erupted along a 110-kilometer stretch of the same border. Tajik forces deployed tanks, armored personnel carriers, and mortars. Kyrgyzstan used armed drones. Kyrgyzstan reported 59 dead and 198 wounded; Tajikistan reported 41 dead. More than 130,000 people were displaced, according to the Jamestown Foundation. Human Rights Watch documented attacks on homes, schools, and medical facilities that it said may constitute war crimes.

A Tentative Shift

The picture has changed since 2016, when Uzbekistan's President Shavkat Mirziyoyev took office and adopted a markedly different approach from his predecessor. Mirziyoyev backed the Rogun Dam rather than opposing it, and in 2024, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Kazakhstan agreed to jointly finance the Kambarata-1 project despite its being located entirely in Kyrgyzstan, the Christian Science Monitor reported. In February 2025, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan signed a border delimitation agreement resolving a dispute dating back to 1991, according to the Jamestown Foundation. The agreement included separate provisions on the division of water resources.

The International Crisis Group, which had spent years documenting the risk of open conflict over water in the region, noted that the four Central Asian states involved had, at various points, come close enough to war that international intervention was seriously discussed. The shift in Uzbekistan's posture under Mirziyoyev removed the most significant political obstacle to regional cooperation, though it did not resolve the underlying physical constraints.

What Climate Change Adds

The diplomatic progress is happening against a deteriorating physical backdrop. Glaciers in the Tian Shan mountains of Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan contribute roughly 75 percent of all the water in the Aral Sea basin, which supplies much of Central Asia, according to the Interstate Commission for Water Coordination. Those glaciers are melting at a rate faster than the global average. In the near term, accelerated melting is temporarily increasing river flows. In the longer term, the water supply will decline, according to the Atlantic Council. The 2021 Central Asian drought gave the region a preview: when water levels dropped, the reservoir feeding the Rogun Dam fell with them, Tajikistan's electricity output declined, and upstream-downstream tensions rose again.

The 2025 border agreement and the new cooperative financing arrangements for Kambarata-1 represent the most progress Central Asia has made on water in three decades. They were also signed before the glaciers finish melting. How those frameworks hold when there is genuinely less water to divide is the question the region has bought itself time to answer—but has not yet answered.
