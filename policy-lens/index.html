EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

For most of the period following the 2020 Galwan Valley clash, China-India relations were defined by what they lacked: high-level visits, direct flights, completed disengagement at key friction points, and even a shared understanding of the border dispute. That changed in 2025. A series of disengagement agreements, ministerial exchanges, and an August 2025 summit between Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Tianjin, the first visit by an Indian leader to China in seven years, produced the most substantial normalization of bilateral relations since 2020.

This brief argues that the thaw is real but limited. It has reduced immediate border tensions, restored diplomatic channels, and reopened selected economic flows. It has not resolved the territorial dispute, narrowed the trade imbalance, or altered the strategic conditions that contributed to the 2020 crisis, particularly China’s partnership with Pakistan and India’s response to it. The May 2025 India-Pakistan conflict, in which Chinese-supplied weapons and reported intelligence support played a role on Pakistan’s side, unfolded in the middle of the thaw without derailing it. That outcome reveals both the resilience and the limits of the current arrangement. At the same time, India’s trade deficit with China reached a record USD 112 to 116 billion in fiscal year 2025-26, even as China became India’s largest trading partner for the first time in several years.

The emerging relationship is neither the unrestrained rivalry many expected after 2020 nor the cooperative partnership some hoped the thaw might produce. It is better understood as a working arrangement between two governments that have concluded they cannot afford open conflict but have not resolved the causes of their confrontation. External pressure, especially the Trump administration’s tariff policy, has made accommodation more attractive to both sides than it was two years earlier. This brief examines the structure of that arrangement and recommends five areas in which regional and international actors can help convert a tactical pause into a more durable framework for managing the relationship’s underlying tensions.

I. THE BORDER: FROM CRISIS TO MANAGED AMBIGUITY

The modern China-India border dispute is rooted in boundaries drawn by colonial powers that neither government fully accepted. The McMahon Line, proposed at the 1914 Simla Convention between British India and Tibet, forms the basis of India’s claim in the eastern sector. China rejects the line’s legitimacy and claims Arunachal Pradesh as part of what it calls “South Tibet.” In the western sector, both countries claim Aksai Chin, which is administered by China but regarded by India as part of Ladakh. These disputes led to the 1962 Sino-Indian War, after which China retained control of Aksai Chin. The two sides then settled into an uneasy frontier governed not by an agreed international border but by the Line of Actual Control (LAC), a working boundary whose precise alignment remains contested.

The June 2020 Galwan Valley clash was the most serious rupture in that arrangement in more than four decades. Twenty Indian soldiers were killed in hand-to-hand combat involving improvised weapons, and China later acknowledged casualties on its side. The clash was followed by years of military buildup along the LAC, including new roads, airfields, surveillance infrastructure, and troop deployments. Diplomatic contact largely froze, while the Indian government imposed restrictions on Chinese investment and mobile applications.

The first signs of movement appeared in 2024. In September, Indian External Affairs Minister Subrahmanyam Jaishankar said that roughly three-quarters of the disengagement problems along the border had been resolved. In October, the two sides reached an agreement covering the Depsang and Demchok sectors, among the last major friction points remaining from the 2020 standoff. India’s Ministry of External Affairs subsequently confirmed that the agreement had been implemented.

Momentum continued into 2025. The two governments agreed to pursue a sector-by-sector approach to the broader boundary question, expressed interest in “early harvest” agreements, and returned to the framework established by the 2005 political parameters agreement, which had been largely dormant since 2020.

This approach partially separates border management from the broader bilateral relationship. It does not resolve the dispute, but it creates mechanisms intended to prevent the dispute from determining the overall state of relations. That is a meaningful departure from the post-2020 period, when border tensions effectively blocked progress in other areas.

The change should not be mistaken for a strategic settlement. The underlying territorial claims remain intact, the military infrastructure built since 2020 remains in place, and the “early harvest” approach itself signals that the most difficult questions, including the final delimitation of Aksai Chin and Arunachal Pradesh, have been deferred. China has also continued to reinforce its claim over Arunachal Pradesh through periodic renaming campaigns. India’s response has remained consistent: formal rejection followed by a return to the status quo. This preserves India’s legal position but does little to interrupt the gradual normalization of China’s cartographic claims.

II. THE 2025 THAW: VISITS, FLIGHTS, AND RARE EARTHS

The diplomatic calendar of 2025 was unusually active for a relationship that had experienced almost no high-level contact since 2020. India’s defense minister visited China in June, followed by the external affairs minister in July. Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi then traveled to New Delhi in August, where the two sides announced several bilateral agreements.

The sequence culminated on August 31, 2025, when Modi attended the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit in Tianjin and met Xi. It was Modi’s first visit to China in seven years and only his second meeting with Xi since their encounter at the BRICS summit in Kazan in October 2024.

The substantive outcomes were limited but significant. Direct passenger flights, suspended since 2020, resumed. India relaxed visa restrictions for Chinese tourists and business travelers. The Kailash-Mansarovar pilgrimage route, a recurring instrument of bilateral goodwill diplomacy, reopened. China also resumed exports to India of tunnel-boring machines, fertilizers such as urea, and, most importantly, rare earth magnets and materials. Beijing had restricted some of these exports earlier in the year as part of a broader tightening of rare earth controls in response to United States trade measures.

In March 2026, India went further by amending Press Note 3, a 2020 regulation requiring government approval for investments from countries that share a land border with India. Although formally applicable to several states, the measure had been aimed primarily at Chinese investment. The amendment eased restrictions for Chinese investment in selected sectors.

Two features of this sequence deserve emphasis. First, its timing cannot be separated from the deterioration in United States trade relations under the second Trump administration. Neither Beijing nor New Delhi has presented the thaw as a strategic reconciliation. Analysts have instead described it as a “partnership of necessity,” shaped by shared external pressure rather than the resolution of bilateral disagreements.

Second, the thaw proved more durable than many observers expected. It survived a direct test in May 2025 that, under earlier conditions, might have frozen the relationship once again.

III. THE MAY 2025 STRESS TEST

On April 22, 2025, gunmen killed 26 people, most of them Indian tourists, in Pahalgam in Indian-administered Kashmir. The attack triggered a four-day military conflict between India and Pakistan from May 7 to 10. It was the most serious confrontation between the two states in years and involved cross-border airstrikes, missile exchanges, and the suspension of the Indus Waters Treaty and the Simla Agreement, two foundational instruments governing India-Pakistan relations since 1960 and 1972, respectively.

China publicly called for de-escalation, but its role extended beyond diplomatic neutrality. A research group affiliated with India’s Ministry of Defense assessed that China had helped Pakistan reorganize radar, air-defense, and satellite coverage before the conflict. If accurate, this assistance would have gone well beyond the provision of weapons. Pakistan’s air force also used Chinese-made J-10C fighters and PL-15 air-to-air missiles. The extent of their success against Indian aircraft remains disputed and has not been independently verified.

Analysts at the Belfer Center and other institutions described the confrontation as the first major real-world test of advanced Chinese military hardware against Western-supplied systems. The implications extended beyond South Asia. The United States-China Economic and Security Review Commission’s 2025 annual report stated that Beijing used the conflict to demonstrate the sophistication of its weapons systems to potential export markets in Africa, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia. The conflict also served China’s strategic interest in maintaining a Pakistan capable of diverting Indian military attention toward a second front.

Indian officials disputed parts of this assessment. Prime Minister Modi maintained that Operation Sindoor had achieved all of its intended objectives. These competing national accounts illustrate how interpretations of the conflict’s military outcome have themselves become instruments of strategic communication.

For China-India relations, the most significant fact may be what did not happen: the thaw did not collapse. During the conflict, China’s foreign minister called for restraint from both sides and supported an early ceasefire. Within months, the Tianjin summit proceeded as planned.

This sequence suggests that the current normalization operates on a separate track from the underlying strategic competition. India and China can reduce tensions along their shared border while China continues to arm and support Pakistan in ways that directly shape India’s security planning. The May 2025 episode showed that such compartmentalization is possible. It did not establish that the arrangement would survive a longer conflict or one with substantially higher casualties.

China’s partnership with Pakistan is reinforced by the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), the flagship South Asian project of the Belt and Road Initiative. CPEC includes the development of Gwadar Port and infrastructure passing through Gilgit-Baltistan, territory that India considers part of the disputed Kashmir region. India has repeatedly protested the corridor as a violation of its sovereignty.

According to data from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, China has been Pakistan’s dominant arms supplier for more than a decade. The May 2025 conflict offered the clearest demonstration yet of what that supplier relationship means for Indian security planning. From New Delhi’s perspective, the structural problem remains unchanged: India faces a potential two-front challenge, and the front involving China’s closest regional partner remains active even when the LAC itself is relatively quiet.

IV. THE ECONOMIC PICTURE: RECORD TRADE, RECORD DEFICIT

The economic relationship between China and India both supports and complicates the diplomatic thaw. In fiscal year 2025-26, China overtook the United States to become India’s largest trading partner for the first time in several years. Bilateral trade reached approximately USD 151 billion. India’s exports to China grew by nearly 37 percent from the previous year, although from a relatively low base. Imports from China also increased, widening India’s trade deficit to a record USD 112 to 116 billion, depending on the methodology used, compared with roughly USD 99 billion the year before.

The composition of trade helps explain why the imbalance has been so difficult to reduce. According to the Global Trade Research Initiative, nearly 80 percent of India’s imports from China fall into four categories: electronics, machinery, organic chemicals, and plastics. These goods are difficult to replace quickly, regardless of the political relationship. India’s exports to China remain concentrated in raw and semi-processed materials, including iron ore, light naphtha, p-xylene, shrimp, and castor oil.

The result is an unequal value-chain relationship. India exports comparatively lower-value raw and semi-processed goods while importing higher-value manufactured inputs that are essential to domestic production. India’s economy may be growing faster than China’s, but its industrial dependence on Chinese components remains substantial.

China’s global trade surplus reached a record USD 1.19 trillion during the year, roughly equivalent to the annual output of a top-20 global economy. This occurred even as Chinese exports to the United States fell by nearly 15 percent under Trump-era tariffs. Beijing responded by redirecting exports toward Southeast Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the European Union, all of which recorded double-digit growth in imports from China.

India’s rising imports fit this broader pattern. As access to the United States market becomes more constrained, India’s large consumer base and limited domestic capacity in electronics and industrial inputs make it an increasingly important destination for Chinese goods.

This creates a central tension in Indian policy. The same fiscal year in which India relaxed Press Note 3 restrictions on Chinese investment also produced the country’s largest-ever trade deficit with China. Indian officials have presented the revised investment rules as a way to move Chinese manufacturing capacity into India and reduce the deficit through local production rather than import restrictions.

That approach carries its own strategic ambiguity. Greater Chinese investment could help India develop domestic manufacturing capacity and reduce finished-goods imports over time. It could also deepen Indian dependence on Chinese capital, components, and technology. The outcome will depend on whether investment is structured to build local capabilities or merely relocates the final stages of production while leaving critical upstream dependencies intact.

V. THE WIDER FIELD: THE QUAD, THE INDIAN OCEAN, AND STRATEGIC AUTONOMY

The thaw has unfolded alongside India’s expanding relationships with other major powers, especially the United States. In the decade following the Galwan clash, India and the United States deepened defense cooperation through joint exercises, intelligence-sharing arrangements, and participation in the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue with Japan and Australia. Shared concern about Chinese assertiveness was an important driver of this cooperation, even as India resisted descriptions of the Quad as a formal alliance.

The Trump administration’s tariff measures against India in 2025 complicated this trajectory. Several analysts linked Washington’s trade pressure on New Delhi to the timing of the Tianjin summit. From this perspective, the China-India thaw represents a hedge against United States unpredictability rather than a broader Indian shift away from Washington.

India’s long-standing commitment to “strategic autonomy” has therefore acquired a new application. During the Cold War, the concept referred primarily to avoiding formal alignment with either the United States or the Soviet Union. Today, it increasingly describes India’s effort to navigate between a powerful but less predictable United States and a neighboring China with which it has unresolved territorial disputes and deep economic interdependence.

Competition also continues across South Asia and the Indian Ocean. China and India are seeking influence through infrastructure financing, port development, and diplomatic outreach, with Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal, and the Maldives among the primary arenas. Indian analysts have repeatedly raised concerns about the potential military use of Chinese-financed port infrastructure, most notably Sri Lanka’s Hambantota Port.

The 2025 thaw has not substantially changed this dimension of the relationship. Both countries appear to treat regional competition as a separate track from bilateral diplomacy, just as Beijing has sought to separate its partnership with Pakistan from its management of relations with India.

VI. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following recommendations are directed toward regional governments, multilateral institutions, and external powers with an interest in stable China-India relations. They begin from a central premise: the 2025 thaw is a genuine improvement over the post-2020 freeze, but it has not addressed the structural sources of volatility. The absence of an immediate crisis should not be confused with a durable settlement.

1. Institutionalize Border De-escalation Before the Window Closes

The 2024-25 disengagement agreements and the decision to pursue a sector-by-sector, “early harvest” approach represent the most important procedural progress on the China-India border since 2005. History shows, however, that such progress can be reversed when the broader political relationship deteriorates. After the 2020 clash, years of dialogue and confidence-building measures were effectively overwhelmed by a single violent confrontation.

Both governments should therefore convert the current disengagement arrangements into standing institutions before diplomatic momentum fades. Priorities should include permanent military hotlines at the corps commander level, regularly scheduled border talks rather than meetings convened only during crises, and a joint mapping exercise beginning with the least-contested sectors of the LAC.

Track-two dialogue platforms and regional institutions can support this process by developing technical proposals, sustaining communication during political downturns, and documenting areas of procedural agreement. The 2005 political parameters framework took 15 years to produce limited results and was largely abandoned after 2020. A narrower approach, built around specific sectors and permanent institutional mechanisms, is more likely to survive the next period of bilateral tension.

2. Address the Trade Imbalance Through Diversification and Domestic Capacity, Not Restriction Alone

India’s record trade deficit with China is not primarily the result of policy choices that can be reversed through investment restrictions. The measures introduced in 2020 did not materially change the deficit’s trajectory, which continued to widen. The imbalance reflects India’s position in regional manufacturing value chains, especially in electronics, machinery, and chemical inputs for which domestic substitutes do not yet exist at scale.

India’s decision to relax Press Note 3 restrictions offers one possible response by encouraging Chinese manufacturers to produce inside India. That approach should be paired with a more targeted diversification strategy designed to reduce dependence on any single supplier of critical inputs.

ASEAN states, Japan, and South Korea can serve as alternative partners in some of the electronics and machinery categories that account for much of the deficit. Trade and investment negotiations with these partners should focus on specific products identified as structurally difficult to replace rather than relying on broad liberalization measures disconnected from India’s most important vulnerabilities.

Diversifying suppliers is not the same as reducing structural dependence. Moving imports from China to Southeast Asian producers that themselves rely heavily on Chinese components may reduce direct exposure without changing the underlying supply chain. The more durable solution is to build domestic capacity in India’s most vulnerable import categories. This will require more time and capital, but it is the only strategy that addresses the structural imbalance rather than redistributing it.

3. Keep the Pakistan Dimension Separate From Crisis Management, but Place It on the Diplomatic Agenda

The fact that the May 2025 India-Pakistan conflict did not derail the China-India thaw demonstrates that Beijing and New Delhi can compartmentalize the Pakistan issue from the rest of their bilateral relationship. In the short term, this has stabilizing value. It allows progress on the border to continue without making every India-Pakistan crisis a veto over China-India diplomacy.

Compartmentalization, however, also allows a central source of strategic distrust to remain unaddressed. Future high-level dialogues should therefore include a standing track on regional security that explicitly considers third-country dynamics. This track should proceed alongside bilateral cooperation rather than serve as a precondition for it.

External actors, including the United States and the European Union, should also avoid assessing the China-India thaw and the China-Pakistan partnership as unrelated developments. The May 2025 conflict demonstrated that the two tracks are strategically connected, even when Beijing and New Delhi prefer to manage them separately.

China has limited incentive to enter a bilateral discussion framed explicitly around Pakistan. A more practical starting point may therefore be an existing multilateral forum in which both states participate, such as the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation. The initial agenda could focus on regional crisis prevention and military transparency rather than on China-Pakistan relations directly.

4. Build Early Warning Systems That Do Not Depend on Political Goodwill

The 2020 Galwan crisis and the rapid escalation of the May 2025 India-Pakistan conflict reveal a common weakness: bilateral communication mechanisms are least reliable when political trust is lowest. Hotlines may exist, but their effectiveness depends on leaders choosing to use them at the moment of greatest tension.

A more practical intervention is to establish shared early warning procedures rather than rely solely on discretionary crisis communication or third-party mediation. China and India should agree on a limited set of escalation indicators, such as unusual troop concentrations in designated LAC sectors, prolonged suspension of border personnel meetings, or communications blackouts lasting beyond an agreed threshold.

Each indicator should trigger an automatic notification requirement. Such a system would not constrain either government’s sovereign response. It would simply ensure that both sides have access to the same basic information before a localized incident becomes a broader confrontation.

The technical architecture could be developed through the existing corps commander-level talks, where an institutional channel already exists. The primary obstacle is not technical capacity but political agreement to make notification automatic rather than optional.

5. Monitor Dual-Use Risk in Belt and Road Infrastructure Without Treating All Investment as Equivalent

Concerns about the potential military use of Chinese-financed ports in the Indian Ocean are well established. Hambantota and comparable projects in Bangladesh, the Maldives, and elsewhere remain central to regional debate. The 2025 thaw and the possible expansion of Chinese investment make it increasingly important to distinguish legitimate commercial infrastructure from projects with credible dual-use implications.

Treating all Belt and Road investment as a security threat risks alienating governments with genuine infrastructure needs and few financing alternatives. At the same time, failing to distinguish between ordinary commercial development and strategically significant projects can normalize arrangements that create long-term security vulnerabilities.

Recipient governments should develop transparent assessment frameworks modeled on debt-sustainability and dual-use review tools already used in part by multilateral lenders. These frameworks should evaluate project financing, ownership terms, location, technical specifications, access rights, and provisions for future conversion or military use.

The goal should not be to block Chinese investment categorically. It should be to allow commercially viable projects to proceed while identifying and managing projects whose design or contractual terms create credible strategic risks.

VII. CONCLUSION

China-India relations in mid-2026 are more stable than at any point since 2020, but far less resolved than the diplomatic atmosphere of the past year might suggest. The border has moved from active crisis to managed ambiguity. Direct flights have resumed, rare earth exports have restarted, and the two leaders have met twice after years of limited contact.

None of these developments required either government to abandon its territorial claims. Nor did they prevent China from supplying Pakistan with weapons, and reportedly intelligence support, during a near-war with India only months before the Tianjin summit.

The underlying structure of the relationship remains intact. What has changed is the cost-benefit calculation surrounding its management. A more volatile global trade environment has given both Beijing and New Delhi stronger incentives to contain bilateral friction. India needed to stabilize ties with its largest trading partner while its relationship with the United States was under strain. China needed to preserve access to major markets as trade with the United States became more constrained.

The resulting arrangement represents genuine progress, but progress in managing a rivalry is not the same as resolving it. Its durability will depend partly on factors neither government fully controls, including the direction of United States trade policy, political stability in Pakistan, and the circumstances of the next border incident or regional crisis.

The greatest risk may not be a dramatic collapse. It may be a gradual return to fragility in which neither side uses the current period of relative calm to build durable institutions. Without stronger border procedures, reliable crisis communication, and a more resilient economic relationship, the next confrontation could expose how little the underlying structure has changed.

The most accurate description of the current relationship is therefore not reconciliation, but managed rivalry. China and India have not resolved their disagreements. For now, they have decided that controlling them is more useful than escalating them. Whether that choice survives the next serious test will depend less on the goodwill displayed in Tianjin than on the institutions both governments build while that goodwill still exists.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Asia’s garment supply chains generate substantial value for global fashion brands, yet much of the social and economic cost falls on workers with the least power to resist. This brief examines labor vulnerability in four major garment-producing economies: Bangladesh, Cambodia, Myanmar, and Vietnam. Brands set prices and delivery schedules, suppliers absorb the resulting pressure, and workers bear it through low wages, unpaid or excessive overtime, insecure contracts, and restrictions on organizing.

More than a decade after the Rana Plaza disaster, factory safety has improved in parts of the region, while progress on living wages, freedom of association, purchasing practices, and legal accountability has been far more limited. The European Union (EU) Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) entered into force in July 2024, but the Omnibus I amendments, completed in February 2026, narrowed its reach to the largest companies and removed its EU-wide civil liability regime. In June 2025, the International Labour Organization (ILO) invoked Article 33 of its Constitution against Myanmar. The timing matters: member states are now transposing the amended directive, and the EU’s revised trade preference scheme, which takes effect in January 2027 as Bangladesh prepares to leave least developed country status, opens a defined window for trade-linked labor commitments.

This brief argues that audits of direct suppliers cannot, on their own, address labor exploitation in the garment sector. Effective reform has to reach below the first tier of suppliers, regulate the purchasing practices of global brands, protect and include workers in due diligence, tie trade benefits to freedom of association, and guarantee wages and severance when factories close. The analysis draws on documentary research and on APEX interviews with three scholars of global labor standards, Professor Tim Bartley of Georgetown University, Professor Mark Anner of Rutgers University, and Professor Sarosh C. Kuruvilla of Cornell University, whose views are attributed to them throughout.

I. THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE PROBLEM

Global apparel production is organized through competitive sourcing. International brands rarely own the factories that manufacture their products. They contract with suppliers, which may in turn subcontract work to additional facilities further down the production chain. Brands negotiate prices, quantities, and delivery schedules, and factories are expected to meet those terms despite fluctuating material, energy, and labor costs.

In a labor-intensive industry with thin supplier margins, the gap between what a brand pays and what production costs is often recovered from workers. Factories may suppress base wages, withhold overtime payments, rely on temporary contracts, raise production quotas, or resist union formation. Mark Anner, Dean and Distinguished Professor at the School of Management and Labor Relations at Rutgers University, explained in an APEX interview that because buildings and equipment are fixed costs, wages and work intensity become the adjustable part of production. When an outright pay cut would be too visible, pressure shows up instead in faster work, unpaid overtime, and lobbying to keep minimum wages behind inflation, and order spikes push overflow work to subcontractors.1 These dynamics allow global brands to benefit from low production costs while keeping a legal distance from the employment conditions that make those costs possible.

Wage gaps across the sector are substantial. According to The Industry We Want coalition’s 2025 Wage Gap Metric, the average gap between legal minimum wages and living wages across 31 key garment-producing countries stands at 41 percent, an improvement from 49.5 percent in 2024 but still far short of a living wage.2 The Asia Floor Wage Alliance’s 2024 survey of more than 3,000 workers across eight Asian garment-producing countries found severe wage shortfalls in every country studied. In Bangladesh and the Philippines, workers earned only 19 percent of the estimated living wage, while the gaps in Cambodia and Myanmar were only marginally smaller.3

Corporate transparency remains limited as well. Fashion Revolution’s Fashion Transparency Index found that 99 percent of major fashion brands do not adequately disclose how many workers in their supply chains receive a living wage, and fewer than one-quarter explain the prevalence of modern slavery risks within their production networks.4

Competition among producing countries adds a further constraint. As Anner summarized it, “That hypermobility and hypercompetitiveness works to the disadvantage of workers.” Because garments can be made almost anywhere, governments weighing a minimum wage increase are routinely warned that orders will move elsewhere.5

II. BANGLADESH: SAFETY REFORM WITHOUT LABOR JUSTICE

Bangladesh’s garment sector employs approximately four million workers and generates most of the country’s export earnings. The 2013 collapse of Rana Plaza, which killed more than 1,100 people, brought global attention to factory safety and labor rights in the industry.6

The reforms that followed produced measurable results. The Bangladesh Accord on Fire and Building Safety established a legally binding inspection system covering more than 1,600 factories and improving conditions for approximately two million workers.7 The International Labour Organization has recognized meaningful progress in occupational safety infrastructure while identifying continuing weaknesses in labor law reform, union rights, and social protection.8

The limits of safety-centered reform became clear during Bangladesh’s 2023 wage dispute. After protests in which at least two workers were killed and dozens were injured, the government raised the statutory monthly minimum wage from Bangladeshi taka (Tk) 8,000 to Tk 12,500, or approximately USD $115.9 Workers had demanded approximately USD $208 per month, arguing that the approved increase was insufficient to cover rising food, rent, and transportation costs. The Asia Floor Wage Alliance estimates that a living wage for Bangladeshi garment workers is approximately Tk 23,000 to 25,000 per month, or USD $210 to $230, nearly twice the statutory minimum.10 As of August 2026, the entry-level minimum remained Tk 12,500, and garment unions were calling for a new Minimum Wage Board and a fresh review of wages against inflation.11

Factory owners also pointed to pressure from international buyers. The president of the Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association said after the increase that it was now buyers’ responsibility to raise product prices, and several unnamed owners told a Dhaka newspaper that buyers had not done so, though the report did not independently document the brands’ positions.12 The tension is a familiar one: brands publicly require suppliers to meet labor standards while using their purchasing power to set prices that make compliance harder.

Professor Tim Bartley of Georgetown University’s Earth Commons and Department of Sociology points to another mechanism reinforcing this imbalance: the short duration and mobility of sourcing relationships. Drawing on his research in Indonesia, he observed in an APEX interview that “orders are short term,” and that buyers can shift factories from one place to another, which “undermines the possibilities for real improvement.” Labor remediation often requires sustained cooperation over time; frequent supplier switching can therefore weaken both accountability and the capacity to solve persistent workplace problems.13

The legal response to the wage protests offers one of the region’s clearest examples of labor repression. Amnesty International reported in May 2024 that at least 35 criminal cases had been filed against garment workers following the demonstrations. The complaints named approximately 161 workers directly and included an estimated 35,900 to 44,450 unnamed workers. Twenty-five of the 35 cases were filed by factories believed to supply major international fashion brands.14

Union leaders told Amnesty International that complaints naming tens of thousands of unidentified workers act as a continuing threat that discourages organizing and wage claims.15 A November 2025 Amnesty International report based on 88 interviews in four South Asian countries concluded that brands, factory owners, and governments had allowed the industry to rely for decades on an underpaid, predominantly female workforce systematically denied freedom of association.16

Bangladesh thus shows the limits of reform that addresses buildings without changing the commercial and legal arrangements surrounding employment.

III. CAMBODIA: DEBT AS A FORM OF LABOR CONTROL

Cambodia’s garment and footwear sector generates approximately 80 percent of the country’s export income and supports about one-fifth of Cambodian households. Conditions in the sector therefore bear directly on national social and economic policy.17

The 2024 Fair Work Monitor, produced by CNV Internationaal, found a median total monthly expenditure of USD $412 among surveyed workers, more than double the statutory minimum wage of USD $204 in force that year.18 The Anker Research Institute put a gross living wage for urban Cambodia at USD $232 per month in 2024 and estimated that an urban household needs approximately USD $417 per month for a basic but decent standard of living. The statutory minimum was USD $208 in 2025 and was set at USD $210 for 2026.19 A September 2025 joint statement by Cambodian trade unions and civil society organizations called for at least USD $232, which it described as the minimum necessary adjustment for 2026 and which would still fall short of household living costs.20

Persistent wage shortfalls have produced widespread debt. According to the 2024 Fair Work Monitor, 73 percent of surveyed Cambodian garment workers had borrowed money to meet basic expenses, down from 77 percent in 2023. The median amount borrowed nonetheless rose from $5,000 in 2023 to $6,000 in 2024 (the monitor does not state the currency). Thirty percent of workers reported running out of food in their household before the end of the month, 32 percent could not afford prescribed glasses, and 42 percent lacked money for medication.21

Anner linked this debt directly to low wages and suggested that cooperatives or public lending at humane interest rates could accompany wage increases, since the immediate question for indebted workers is who they borrow from and at what rate.22

Debt also shapes workplace power. Workers carrying large debts are less able to risk dismissal, a strike, or an interruption in wages, which limits their practical freedom to organize, refuse unsafe work, or leave abusive employment.

The value generated is considerable. Cambodia’s garment and footwear exports were worth at least USD $11 billion in 2022, while brands sourcing there, including H&M, Bestseller, and Next PLC, reported profits of hundreds of millions of dollars that year.23

Cambodia also exposes the weakness of factory-level auditing as an accountability mechanism. During the COVID-19 period, the Violet Apparel factory in Cambodia and the Hong Seng Knitting factory in Thailand closed without paying required severance. Investors and labor rights advocates called on Nike, whose products had been manufactured at both facilities, to compensate more than 4,000 workers for a combined USD $2.2 million in unpaid wages and benefits.24

Nike did not own the factories and, under conventional supply chain law, had no direct legal obligation to compensate the workers, which leaves a brand able to profit from workers’ labor for years while remaining legally insulated when they go unpaid.

IV. MYANMAR: WHEN POLITICAL REPRESSION ENTERS THE FACTORY

Myanmar is the most severe case examined in this brief. Garment workers face the low wages, excessive overtime, and anti-union practices found elsewhere in the region, and they also operate under military rule and the threat of direct state violence.

Following the February 2021 military coup, the Business & Human Rights Resource Centre recorded 665 allegations of garment worker abuse across 304 factories through October 2024. These factories had produced goods for approximately 187 identified global brands and retailers, including Inditex, H&M Group, Primark, and Bestseller.25

The most frequently reported abuses included mandatory overtime, escalating production quotas, wage deductions, gender-based harassment, and retaliation against union members. More than half of the recorded cases involved one or more of these practices.26 In at least one documented incident, security forces used lethal force during a protest and killed a woman union leader.27

Myanmar’s implementation of the People’s Military Service Law in 2024 created an additional threat. Workers reported fearing forced conscription while returning home after mandatory late shifts. Excessive overtime could therefore add a risk of military recruitment to the risk of exploitation, a danger without a clear equivalent in other major garment-producing economies.28

The international response has been significant but incomplete. In June 2025, the 113th International Labour Conference invoked Article 33 of the ILO Constitution against Myanmar, the third use of the measure in the organization’s history and the first time a country had faced it twice. The resolution, adopted by consensus although China, Russia, Belarus, and Laos voiced objections, called for disengagement from military-linked businesses and cited violations of Conventions No. 87 and No. 29.29

The use of Article 33 is historically significant, and it also creates a policy dilemma. Broad corporate withdrawal can reduce revenue available to the military, but it can also eliminate civilian employment if it fails to distinguish between military-linked and independently owned factories; the EU, in supporting the resolution, stressed the need to avoid measures that could adversely affect the people of Myanmar.30 Several brands, including Marks & Spencer, announced exits, and an assessment commissioned by the Ethical Trading Initiative concluded that companies could not conduct due diligence in Myanmar consistent with international responsible business standards.31 Such exits may be justified where meaningful oversight is impossible, yet withdrawal alone provides no accountability.

The 665 documented allegations arose within supply chains in which brands maintained codes of conduct, sustainability policies, and supplier agreements. The failure of these instruments under military rule shows the weakness of corporate standards that lack enforceable remedies.

Kuruvilla identified a broader institutional reason why these systems often fail: “The biggest reason is that companies don’t necessarily follow up when they get an audit report.” He added that many audits are “very perfunctory,” with auditors checking boxes during short visits rather than conducting deep investigations. This suggests that the weakness lies as much in the limited capacity and incentives for sustained remediation as in the formal content of corporate standards.32

Myanmar also exposes a gap in international policy, the absence of clear requirements for responsible disengagement. Brands leaving high-risk environments should be required to verify the payment of outstanding wages and severance, provide advance notice where possible, and contribute to transition funds for affected workers rather than simply ending supplier contracts.

V. VIETNAM: LEGAL STRUCTURES AND THE SUPPRESSION OF WORKER VOICE

Vietnam, a major beneficiary of manufacturing diversification away from China, faces labor rights problems that are less visible than Myanmar’s, and this rests on a legal and political framework that prevents independent worker representation in practice.

Vietnam does not permit genuinely independent unions to operate freely. Its legal framework recognizes organizations affiliated with the Vietnam General Confederation of Labor, which remains under Communist Party oversight. In a May 2024 submission to the United States Department of Commerce, Human Rights Watch described Vietnam’s claim that wages were determined through free bargaining between labor and management as “patently false.” It noted that no independent worker representative organizations existed at the enterprise level in practice at that time.33

The political environment became more restrictive after the Communist Party Politburo issued Directive 24 in mid-2023, instructing officials to monitor unions closely and prevent new independent labor organizations.34 In April and May 2024, police detained the director general of the Ministry of Labor’s legal department, who had worked on ratifying ILO Convention No. 87, and a senior official of the Vietnam General Confederation of Labor.35

Vietnam’s revised Trade Union Law entered into force on July 1, 2025. It introduced several changes, including allowing foreign workers to join trade unions, but left in place the system of union representation centered on the Vietnam General Confederation of Labor. Vietnam has repeatedly pledged to ratify ILO Convention No. 87 but has not done so. Under a side letter between Canada and Vietnam to the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), Canada agreed not to seek suspension of benefits for certain freedom-of-association violations for five years after the agreement entered into force for Vietnam; that grace period expired in January 2024. The side letter did not itself create a five-year deadline for ratification.36

The Asia Floor Wage Alliance’s 2024 survey found that Vietnamese garment workers earned about USD $440 per month including overtime, against a living wage benchmark of about USD $500.37 Workers cannot reach a basic standard of living on regular wages alone, and without independent collective bargaining they have little ability to negotiate higher base pay.

VI. REGULATORY RESPONSES AND THEIR CURRENT LIMITATIONS

The EU’s Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive entered into force on July 25, 2024, as the most significant mandatory supply chain accountability framework adopted by a major importing market and one with direct reach into all four countries examined here.38

Its reach has since been substantially reduced. In April 2025, the EU adopted the Stop-the-Clock Directive, postponing key deadlines.39 The Omnibus I package followed. The European Parliament and the Council of the European Union reached political agreement in December 2025, and the Council gave its final approval on February 24, 2026. The amendments raised the CSDDD threshold to companies with more than 5,000 employees and more than €1.5 billion in net turnover, removed the EU-wide harmonized civil-liability regime, and extended the transposition deadline to July 2028 with company compliance from July 2029.40

The amendments remain contested. The European Commission presented Omnibus I as a simplification package intended to reduce administrative burdens and strengthen European competitiveness. Critics, including the environmental law organization ClientEarth, describe it as deregulation, and in November 2025 the European Ombudsman found that the Commission had committed maladministration by preparing the package without the impact assessment and public consultation normally required.41

The resulting framework places mandatory due diligence on fewer companies and removes the EU-wide harmonized liability regime, leaving civil liability to national rules. A common concern during the Omnibus negotiations was that these changes would reduce due diligence to a simple tier-1 rule. Companies may instead focus on the parts of their chains of activities where actual and potential adverse impacts are most likely to occur and, where risks are equally likely or equally severe, may prioritize impacts involving direct business partners. This preserves multi-tier reach in principle while leaving companies substantial discretion over where scrutiny is concentrated.

Bartley addressed this concern directly. He noted that a proposal centered more narrowly on the first tier did not ultimately become the final approach. Instead, “companies are responsible for the whole supply chain.” Not just the first tier, he explained, but companies are expected to identify where actual and potential adverse impacts are most likely to occur. The vulnerability remains, however, because companies retain substantial discretion over where to focus. As he cautioned, “They have a lot of flexibility to decide where to focus their attention,” particularly in a system without an EU-wide harmonized civil-liability regime.42

Brands may place orders with approved first-tier factories, which then transfer work to smaller or less visible facilities. Conventional audits often fail to detect these arrangements or the abuses occurring within them. A risk-based framework can reach these lower tiers, but its effectiveness depends on whether companies accurately identify severe risks in parts of the supply chain that are difficult for workers, regulators, and brands themselves to observe.

Anner, speaking from knowledge of the industry rather than detailed study of the legislation, warned that the risk-based trigger can become a loophole, since it is unclear who decides that risk exists below the first tier when brands have little incentive to look and unions are often weak. Inspections also gravitate toward showcase facilities. In his words, “You end up with these relatively nice tier-one factories that get inspected. . . but most of the work gets done in tier two and tier three.”43

The Bangladesh Accord offers a contrasting model. Its effectiveness came from three connected features: legally binding obligations, worker and union participation in governance, and enforceable remedies. Voluntary corporate initiatives have generally lacked all three, and a weakened due diligence framework risks reproducing the same deficiencies at the regulatory level.44

Professor Sarosh C. Kuruvilla of Cornell University’s ILR School adds an important limitation to this comparison. In an APEX interview, he noted that the Bangladesh Accord “only focused on one issue, which is fire safety, and not labor standards in general.” Its success therefore shows that binding enforcement can work, but not that the same model automatically resolves wages, freedom of association, or other labor standards.45

VII. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following recommendations are directed toward international institutions, major importing governments, producer countries, and multilateral bodies with standard-setting authority. They address the root causes of labor vulnerability rather than isolated factory-level violations.

These measures carry real costs and risks. Higher compliance costs may push buyers toward consolidation or exit from high-risk countries, producer-country employers have consistently warned that wage increases will drive orders elsewhere, and trade leverage works only when importing governments are willing to use it.46 The recommendations are designed with these constraints in mind: they target individual facilities rather than whole countries, attach obligations to the buyer decisions that generate pressure, and pair new duties with exit rules and shared funding so that the burden does not fall back on workers.

1. Strengthen Risk-Based Multi-Tier Supply Chain Due Diligence

The EU and other jurisdictions developing due diligence laws should ensure that risk-based obligations operate across the full supply chain rather than being treated as a de facto tier-1 exercise. Lower-tier subcontractors should remain within scope wherever risk mapping indicates severe or likely labor abuses, and regulators should scrutinize how companies exercise their discretion in prioritizing those risks.

EU member states should adopt strong national implementation measures wherever the directive permits them to exceed minimum requirements. Regulators should scrutinize how companies conduct risk mapping, especially where evidence is difficult to obtain from less visible suppliers. The United States, United Kingdom, Japan, and other major importing markets should likewise design due diligence requirements around the actual structure of garment production rather than contractual proximity alone.

Companies should be required to map subcontracting relationships, disclose authorized and unauthorized production sites, document the basis for their risk prioritization, and investigate credible risks even when they occur outside a direct contractual relationship. Without these requirements, broad formal coverage can still leave lower-tier workers unprotected if companies use their discretion to overlook difficult-to-observe risks.

Rules should also specify who may trigger scrutiny of lower tiers, so that the decision does not rest on a company’s own assessment. Regulators should accept evidence from unions, worker organizations, and civil society as grounds for investigation.

2. Make Purchasing Practices a Legal Compliance Requirement

A brand cannot credibly demand compliance with labor standards while setting prices and delivery schedules that make compliance financially impossible. Governments and international institutions should treat purchasing practices as part of human rights due diligence rather than as a separate commercial issue.

Bartley’s assessment reinforces the structural logic behind this recommendation. Even where brands publicly commit to responsible sourcing, he argued that “their purchasing practices often make that almost impossible to really live up to.” Squeezing prices and delivery times, repeatedly switching factories, or continuously pushing down prices can undermine the same labor standards that compliance programs are intended to protect. Purchasing practices should therefore be evaluated as part of the causal architecture of labor risk, not merely as a separate commercial choice.47

Anner proposed a concrete test, which he calls total costing: “I think the burden should be on the brands. When they say a dollar ten, show me your math on how that dollar ten covers safe buildings, decent conditions to work, no child labor, no forced labor, occupational health and safety, and living wages.” Garment pricing is difficult to standardize, but complexity cannot become an accountability loophole. As he put it, “Hiding behind the complexity allows these abusive purchasing practices to continue, and workers pay the price.” Regulation therefore need not impose a single garment price; it can require brands to document how their purchasing terms account for lawful wages, working hours, and safety costs. The Bangladesh Accord offers a precedent, since its purchasing clause was backed by binding arbitration.48

The case for making purchasing practices a legal compliance issue is strengthened by the present absence of disclosure obligations. As Kuruvilla explained, “There is nothing at the moment that requires companies to share publicly, or with anybody else, their purchasing practices.” If commercial terms remain confidential by default, regulators and researchers may lack the evidence needed to determine whether price, payment, or deadline decisions contribute to labor violations. A legal framework would therefore need access to the underlying procurement data required to enforce purchasing-practice standards, in addition to the standards themselves.49

Companies covered by due diligence laws should be required to assess whether their prices, payment schedules, order changes, and production deadlines are consistent with living wages and lawful working hours. Brands should disclose purchasing practice data alongside their human rights reports. The International Labour Organization’s work on living wages should inform sector-specific guidance on responsible garment pricing, and trade agreements with major garment-producing countries should address purchasing practices within their labor chapters.

Compliance should be assessed against the buyer’s conduct as well as the factory’s, since a buyer’s own behavior may have made a violation more likely.

3. Require Worker Participation in Due Diligence

Workers and unions often identify labor risks before auditors do. Traditional audits may fail to detect wage suppression, anti-union retaliation, informal subcontracting, or coercive overtime because they depend heavily on records and interviews controlled by factory management.

Workers must therefore participate directly in identifying, monitoring, and remedying supply chain risks. This participation must include enforceable protection against retaliation. In environments where reporting abuse can lead to dismissal, criminal charges, or violence, consultation without legal protection is not meaningful participation. This protection depends substantially on the trade-linked pressure described in Recommendation 4 to create the domestic legal conditions under which worker voice can function without reprisal.

Kuruvilla also cautions that worker participation cannot be separated from the legal conditions under which workers organize. “If unions have to be a stakeholder in the process, they have to be legal and they have to have the freedom to operate,” he argued. Worker-participation mechanisms are therefore meaningful only when workers can organize freely and independently.50

Due diligence systems should include independent worker-led risk assessments alongside conventional audits. Civil society organizations with direct relationships to workers should receive formal rights to submit evidence in regulatory proceedings.

Where local unions are constrained, participation will need outside support at first. Anner’s research points to international union federations such as IndustriALL Global Union and advocacy groups such as the Worker Rights Consortium helping to break an impasse, with their role receding as local organizing grows; this route is far harder in Vietnam, which does not admit international groups.51 Due diligence rules should therefore recognize international union federations and worker rights organizations as eligible stakeholders where local representation is restricted, while treating their role as transitional.

4. Condition Market Access on Genuine Freedom of Association

Freedom of association underpins every other labor standard, since it is the means by which workers defend wages, working hours, safety, maternity protections, and the rest. Vietnam’s restrictions on independent unions, Bangladesh’s mass criminal complaints following wage protests, and Myanmar’s use of state security forces against workers all point to core ILO rights that carry little trade consequence.

The EU, United States, and other major importing markets should use labor provisions in trade agreements, including existing dispute mechanisms, to require measurable progress on freedom of association. Trade preferences for Bangladesh and Cambodia should be linked to concrete action against mass criminal complaints, retaliation, and restrictions on labor organizing.

Trade-linked labor conditions also require more than formal treaty language. Bartley emphasized that progress depends on “the right combination” of a trade partner willing to push for change and an exporting government that is open to at least some degree of reform. This suggests that labor chapters are most credible when importing partners are prepared to use their leverage consistently and when enforcement is paired with institutional capacity-building rather than assuming that commitments will implement themselves.52

The EU’s own trade preferences offer both a precedent and a near-term opportunity. In August 2020, it withdrew Everything But Arms preferences on roughly 20 percent of Cambodia’s exports to the EU, including some garments, footwear, and travel goods, citing serious and systematic human rights concerns.53 Bangladesh was scheduled to graduate from least developed country status in November 2026 but requested a three-year extension; the United Nations Committee for Development Policy and Economic and Social Council recommended it in July 2026, and a General Assembly decision was still pending in late September.54 Under the EU’s new Generalised Scheme of Preferences, which applies from January 1, 2027, a graduating country keeps Everything But Arms preferences for three years, after which continued preferential access through the enhanced GSP Plus arrangement depends on effectively implementing 32 international conventions, including core labor standards. Labor rights violations can also trigger the withdrawal of preferences from any beneficiary.55 Dhaka has asked the EU to relax parts of the revised criteria.56 Whatever graduation date the General Assembly confirms, the transition gives both sides a defined window to agree on measurable labor commitments.

Experience with trade agreements points to a design lesson. Anner judged that labor chapters have mostly fallen short, noting that a case against Guatemala under the Dominican Republic-Central America Free Trade Agreement ran for years and ended in 2017 with a panel ruling against the United States and no sanctions.57 The Rapid Response Labor Mechanism of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement instead sets short deadlines and targets individual facilities; by September 2024 it had been invoked 27 times, and eleven cases had produced independent unions, including at General Motors in Silao and Goodyear in San Luis Potosí.58

Importing governments should build labor-chapter and preference mechanisms on this facility-specific, time-bound model and reserve country-level suspension for documented patterns of repression. In Myanmar, the Article 33 resolution should support coordinated restrictions on goods connected to military-linked enterprises while preserving market access for independently owned civilian producers that meet transparent labor and ownership standards.

5. Establish Mandatory Wage and Severance Guarantee Funds

Factory closures repeatedly expose the same accountability gap. Workers may produce goods for an international brand for years but receive no wages or severance when a supplier becomes insolvent or closes without meeting its obligations.

Major importing governments should require brands sourcing from high-risk jurisdictions to contribute to independently held, sector-level wage and severance guarantee funds, calculated by sourcing volume and supplier risk and released when employers fail to pay legally required wages, benefits, or severance.

Development finance institutions, including the World Bank’s International Finance Corporation, should condition garment-sector financing on supplier participation in such schemes. Producer governments should pair wage reform with regulated, low-interest credit through cooperatives or public programs.

The International Labour Organization and the United Nations Global Compact should also develop standardized responsible exit protocols. Before terminating a supplier relationship in a crisis environment, a brand should be required to assess the effect on workers, verify outstanding wage and severance obligations, provide reasonable notice where possible, and contribute to remediation or transition support. A responsible exit reduces exposure to abuse without abandoning the workers who have already created value for the brand.

VIII. CONCLUSION

Rana Plaza remains the defining reference point for labor exploitation in Asia’s garment sector because its consequences were immediate, visible, and impossible to deny. The industry’s principal response was architectural: make buildings safer. That response worked because the problem was clearly defined, obligations were legally enforceable, and unions took part in implementation.

Wage theft, debt, coercive overtime, and the suppression of organizing are harder to see, and no equally specific or enforceable mechanisms have been applied to them.

Across Bangladesh, Cambodia, Myanmar, and Vietnam, garment workers face inadequate wages, household debt, legal retaliation, restricted freedom of association, and, in Myanmar, direct state violence. Much of the clothing they make is priced as it is because part of the true cost is transferred to them and their families. The three scholars interviewed for this brief approach the problem from different directions. Bartley emphasizes how short-term, mobile sourcing and purchasing behavior erode the possibility of improvement, Anner emphasizes cost transparency, enforceable agreements, and mechanisms that can scale across borders, and Kuruvilla stresses the limits of audits, the narrow scope of the Accord, and the legal conditions that worker participation requires. Read together, their accounts suggest that accountability has to reach the commercial decisions of buyers.

The ILO’s invocation of Article 33 against Myanmar shows that the international community recognizes the severity of the crisis, but it does not by itself create a system capable of compensating workers, regulating brand conduct, or protecting employees affected by corporate withdrawal. The EU’s due diligence framework may still influence global sourcing, and its effect will depend on whether implementing rules reach beyond direct suppliers, examine the purchasing practices of brands, and give workers an enforceable role in identifying and remedying abuse.

Accountability must become as detailed, enforceable, and internationally coordinated as the supply chains that produce the exploitation. At present, it is not.

Anner’s research points to international union federations such as IndustriALL Global Union and advocacy groups such as the Worker Rights Consortium helping to break an impasse, with their role receding as local organizing grows; this route is far harder in Vietnam, which does not admit international groups.51 Due diligence rules should therefore recognize international union federations and worker rights organizations as eligible stakeholders where local representation is restricted, while treating their role as transitional.

4. Condition Market Access on Genuine Freedom of Association

Freedom of association underpins every other labor standard, since it is the means by which workers defend wages, working hours, safety, maternity protections, and the rest. Vietnam’s restrictions on independent unions, Bangladesh’s mass criminal complaints following wage protests, and Myanmar’s use of state security forces against workers all point to core ILO rights that carry little trade consequence.

The EU, United States, and other major importing markets should use labor provisions in trade agreements, including existing dispute mechanisms, to require measurable progress on freedom of association. Trade preferences for Bangladesh and Cambodia should be linked to concrete action against mass criminal complaints, retaliation, and restrictions on labor organizing.

Trade-linked labor conditions also require more than formal treaty language. Bartley emphasized that progress depends on “the right combination” of a trade partner willing to push for change and an exporting government that is open to at least some degree of reform. This suggests that labor chapters are most credible when importing partners are prepared to use their leverage consistently and when enforcement is paired with institutional capacity-building rather than assuming that commitments will implement themselves.52

The EU’s own trade preferences offer both a precedent and a near-term opportunity. In August 2020, it withdrew Everything But Arms preferences on roughly 20 percent of Cambodia’s exports to the EU, including some garments, footwear, and travel goods, citing serious and systematic human rights concerns.53 Bangladesh was scheduled to graduate from least developed country status in November 2026 but requested a three-year extension; the United Nations Committee for Development Policy and Economic and Social Council recommended it in July 2026, and a General Assembly decision was still pending in late September.54 Under the EU’s new Generalised Scheme of Preferences, which applies from January 1, 2027, a graduating country keeps Everything But Arms preferences for three years, after which continued preferential access through the enhanced GSP Plus arrangement depends on effectively implementing 32 international conventions, including core labor standards. Labor rights violations can also trigger the withdrawal of preferences from any beneficiary.55 Dhaka has asked the EU to relax parts of the revised criteria.56 Whatever graduation date the General Assembly confirms, the transition gives both sides a defined window to agree on measurable labor commitments.

Experience with trade agreements points to a design lesson. Anner judged that labor chapters have mostly fallen short, noting that a case against Guatemala under the Dominican Republic-Central America Free Trade Agreement ran for years and ended in 2017 with a panel ruling against the United States and no sanctions.57 The Rapid Response Labor Mechanism of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement instead sets short deadlines and targets individual facilities; by September 2024 it had been invoked 27 times, and eleven cases had produced independent unions, including at General Motors in Silao and Goodyear in San Luis Potosí.58

Importing governments should build labor-chapter and preference mechanisms on this facility-specific, time-bound model and reserve country-level suspension for documented patterns of repression. In Myanmar, the Article 33 resolution should support coordinated restrictions on goods connected to military-linked enterprises while preserving market access for independently owned civilian producers that meet transparent labor and ownership standards.

5. Establish Mandatory Wage and Severance Guarantee Funds

Factory closures repeatedly expose the same accountability gap. Workers may produce goods for an international brand for years but receive no wages or severance when a supplier becomes insolvent or closes without meeting its obligations.

Major importing governments should require brands sourcing from high-risk jurisdictions to contribute to independently held, sector-level wage and severance guarantee funds, calculated by sourcing volume and supplier risk and released when employers fail to pay legally required wages, benefits, or severance.

Development finance institutions, including the World Bank’s International Finance Corporation, should condition garment-sector financing on supplier participation in such schemes. Producer governments should pair wage reform with regulated, low-interest credit through cooperatives or public programs.

The International Labour Organization and the United Nations Global Compact should also develop standardized responsible exit protocols. Before terminating a supplier relationship in a crisis environment, a brand should be required to assess the effect on workers, verify outstanding wage and severance obligations, provide reasonable notice where possible, and contribute to remediation or transition support. A responsible exit reduces exposure to abuse without abandoning the workers who have already created value for the brand.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Five years after the February 2021 military coup, Myanmar remains trapped in a worsening catastrophe. What began as a political rupture, involving the arrest of elected leader Aung San Suu Kyi and the seizure of power by Senior General Min Aung Hlaing's Tatmadaw, has evolved into one of the most severe humanitarian crises in modern Southeast Asian history. In 2025 alone, more than 15,000 people were killed in conflict-related violence, nearly 5.2 million people were displaced, and 22 million required humanitarian assistance. A magnitude 7.7 earthquake in March 2025 further compounded an already catastrophic situation.

The junta's elections, held from December 2025 through January 2026 across only one-third of the country's townships, did not resolve the crisis. Major opposition parties were banned, their leaders remained imprisoned, and airstrikes against civilian populations continued throughout the election period. Rather than restoring democracy, the elections deepened the crisis by creating a facade of democratic legitimacy.

International frameworks have failed to keep pace with the scale of the conflict. ASEAN's Five-Point Consensus has been openly disregarded since it was adopted. The UN Security Council remains deadlocked. The National Unity Government (NUG), the civilian government in exile that commands the loyalty of an overwhelming majority of Myanmar's population, continues to be denied formal international recognition. In April 2026, Suu Kyi was transferred from prison to house arrest as part of a prisoner amnesty associated with a Buddhist holiday. The move was widely interpreted as a political gesture by the newly inaugurated junta president rather than a substantive step toward resolving the conflict.

This brief argues that the international community faces a structural choice. It can continue engaging with the junta through frameworks that have demonstrably failed, or it can adopt a new strategy centered on civilian protection, meaningful engagement with the NUG, and accountability for atrocity crimes. This brief recommends five interventions in support of the latter approach.

I. ORIGINS: FROM ELECTION TO COUP TO CIVIL WAR

Myanmar's civil conflict did not begin in 2021. Armed resistance by ethnic minority groups against a Burman-dominated central government dates back to the country's independence in 1948. A military coup in 1962 subsequently established decades of authoritarian rule. What changed in 2021 was the deliberate destruction of a democratic transition that, although imperfect, had been underway.

The National League for Democracy (NLD), led by Aung San Suu Kyi, won a landslide victory in the November 2020 general election, securing 83 percent of the available parliamentary seats. The military's proxy party, the Union Solidarity and Development Party (USDP), suffered a decisive defeat. The military then claimed, without credible evidence, that the election had been fraudulent.

On February 1, 2021, the day the new parliament was scheduled to convene, Min Aung Hlaing's Tatmadaw seized power, arrested Suu Kyi and President Win Myint, and declared a one-year state of emergency. The state of emergency was repeatedly extended and was not formally lifted until July 31, 2025, four and a half years later, as part of the military's preparations for its staged elections.

The coup triggered an immediate and massive public response. Through the Civil Disobedience Movement, millions of workers, including doctors, teachers, engineers, railway employees, and port workers, took to the streets and withdrew from government service. The Tatmadaw responded with lethal force, killing hundreds of protesters in the weeks following the coup.

By mid-2021, the resistance had become militarized. Ousted civilian legislators formed the National Unity Government and established the People's Defense Forces (PDF) as its armed wing. Existing ethnic armed organizations (EAOs), including the Kachin Independence Army, the Karen National Union, and the Ta'ang National Liberation Army, formed alliances with the resistance.

By late 2023, resistance forces controlled more than half of Myanmar's territory. By 2024, the junta maintained de facto control over only 21 percent of the country's townships, a reversal that few analysts had expected to occur so quickly. However, the military retained several critical asymmetric advantages. These included air power, which it used against civilian infrastructure as a deliberate counterinsurgency tool; external support from China, Russia, and India; and the capacity to maintain control over major urban centers in central Myanmar through a prolonged war of attrition.

As of mid-2026, the result is a stalemate that is far from neutral in its human cost. The side with air power is also the side destroying schools, hospitals, monasteries, and displacement camps.

II. THE POLITICAL TRAP: LEGITIMACY, ELECTIONS, AND THE STATUS OF SUU KYI

Since 2021, the junta's central strategic problem has been its lack of legitimacy. It controls territory through force, but it does not command public consent. The elections held from December 2025 through January 2026 were explicitly designed to address this problem. In the words of an April 2026 joint report by the Special Advisory Council for Myanmar and the Asian Network for Free Elections (ANFREL), the elections sought to "cloak the military junta in civilian garb."

The elections were conducted in three phases and were limited to townships under junta control, representing roughly one-third of the country's 330 townships. The NLD, Myanmar's most popular political party, had been dissolved by the junta in 2023. Its leader, Aung San Suu Kyi, remained detained at an undisclosed location in Naypyitaw.

Most of the 57 registered political parties were either small or aligned with the military. The junta's proxy party, the USDP, was positioned to win. During the election period alone, open-source reporting documented 408 military air attacks that killed at least 170 civilians. In January 2026, a single airstrike on Bhamo Township in Kachin State reportedly killed as many as 50 civilians, despite there being no reported presence of combatants in the area.

In January 2026, Tom Andrews, the UN Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights in Myanmar, called on the international community to unequivocally reject the election results as illegitimate. The United Nations, Human Rights Watch, International IDEA, and more than 300 civil society organizations across the region also rejected the elections. China and Russia, as they have throughout the conflict, provided diplomatic and technical support for the electoral process.

On April 10, 2026, Min Aung Hlaing was sworn in as president under the political system created through these elections. Twenty days later, he announced a prisoner amnesty associated with a Buddhist holiday that converted the remainder of Suu Kyi's sentence to house arrest.

Suu Kyi was photographed for the first time in years, seated on a wooden bench and flanked by uniformed personnel. She is 80 years old. Her legal team has not been permitted to meet with her in person since December 2022.

Burma Campaign UK responded directly: "The Burmese military knows that using Aung San Suu Kyi and other political prisoners is an effective way of gaining international attention and receiving international praise." The timing supports this interpretation. Suu Kyi was transferred to house arrest nineteen days after Min Aung Hlaing's inauguration, at a time when he was seeking renewed engagement with ASEAN. The transfer was a political instrument, not a peace process.

The NUG, which surveys indicate is recognized by 93 percent of Myanmar's population as the country's legitimate government, continues to operate largely in exile. Its September 2025 cabinet meeting was held in Brussels. Its armed wing, the People's Defense Forces, grew from approximately 65,000 fighters in 2022 to around 85,000 by 2024.

Acting President Duwa Lashi La has repeatedly called for cabinet members to operate from liberated areas within Myanmar. However, conditions have not yet allowed the entire leadership to do so safely.

The contest over legitimacy is not abstract. It determines who controls humanitarian access, who participates in negotiations, and which government will inherit the state if Myanmar's crisis eventually produces a negotiated transition.

III. THE HUMANITARIAN CATASTROPHE

Any honest account of Myanmar's crisis must begin with the scale of civilian suffering. That scale makes the failure of the international response especially consequential.

ACLED estimated that more than 15,000 people were killed in conflict-related violence in 2025 alone. Nearly 5.2 million people were displaced, both internally and across international borders, making Myanmar one of the largest displacement crises in Asia.

The UN's 2025 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan requested US$1.1 billion to support 5.5 million people. This appeal was issued against a broader estimate that 19.9 million people would require humanitarian assistance. By November 2025, donors had provided only 15 percent of the required funding. In practice, the 2025 Humanitarian Response Plan became a document defining who would not be reached.

The 2025 Global Peace Index placed Myanmar's conflict alongside the civil wars in South Sudan, Syria, and Mali in terms of conflict escalation and ethnic exclusion. Myanmar was also identified as the world's largest producer of synthetic drugs. This expanding narco-economy strengthens organized crime networks, complicates humanitarian access, and intensifies cycles of violence in border regions.

On March 28, 2025, a magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck central Myanmar. It was the most powerful earthquake to hit the country in more than a century. The disaster devastated the Mandalay and Sagaing regions, as well as the Naypyitaw Union Territory. More than 3,600 people were killed, approximately 5,000 were injured, and more than 10,000 structures, including hospitals, temples, and bridges, were destroyed.

The earthquake struck a population that had already been severely weakened by years of conflict. More than 3 million people had been displaced before the disaster, and nearly 20 million already required humanitarian assistance.

The junta's response to the earthquake exposed the central contradiction in its relationship with the civilian population. It publicly requested international assistance, an unusual step for a government that had spent four years restricting humanitarian access. At the same time, international emergency response teams faced visa denials and delays. Junta officials confiscated medicine, while aid workers were subjected to extortion and harassment. Internet access was blocked, and airstrikes in areas outside junta control continued despite the announcement of a temporary ceasefire.

The humanitarian community therefore found itself attempting to deliver assistance to earthquake survivors while the government requesting that assistance was simultaneously obstructing relief operations and bombing civilian populations in nearby areas.

Following Suu Kyi's transfer to house arrest in April 2026, the UN Secretary-General reiterated his call for "the swift and unconditional release of all those arbitrarily detained as a fundamental step towards essential political reform." He further stated that "a viable political solution must be founded on an immediate end to the brutal civil conflict across Myanmar and a genuine commitment to inclusive dialogue."

The fact that the UN Secretary-General continues to make these statements five years into the conflict is itself a measure of how little meaningful progress has been achieved.

IV. THE FAILURE OF EXISTING FRAMEWORKS

Understanding why the international response has failed requires examining what existing frameworks were designed to accomplish and identifying the structural constraints that prevented them from succeeding.

ASEAN's Five-Point Consensus was adopted in April 2021 by ASEAN member states and Min Aung Hlaing himself. It called for an immediate cessation of violence, constructive dialogue among all parties, the appointment of a special envoy, the provision of humanitarian assistance, and a visit by the envoy to Myanmar to meet with all relevant parties.

Four years later, none of these commitments had been meaningfully implemented. The junta had openly violated every provision. ASEAN member states remained divided. Thailand and Cambodia continued to engage with the junta, while Malaysia and Indonesia pushed for the inclusion of the NUG. ASEAN's consensus-based model, which requires unanimity, ensured that the lowest common denominator prevailed.

In April 2025, Malaysia, serving as ASEAN Chair, held the first virtual meeting between ASEAN leadership and NUG representatives. This was a significant step, but it followed four years of institutional exclusion of the civilian government that most of Myanmar's population recognizes as legitimate.

In its July 2025 statement on the ASEAN Foreign Ministers' Meeting, the NUG acknowledged ASEAN's response to the earthquake. However, it also expressed "deep disappointment" at ASEAN's continued failure to explicitly condemn the junta's ongoing war crimes or adopt enforcement mechanisms with meaningful consequences.

The UN Security Council has been structurally unable to take effective action. China and Russia, both of which have commercial and strategic interests in maintaining cooperation with the junta, have blocked binding measures.

In June 2025, the International Labour Organization invoked Article 33 of its Constitution against Myanmar. This marked only the third use of Article 33 in the organization's history and the second time it had been invoked against Myanmar. The action cited violations of Convention No. 87 on freedom of association and Convention No. 29 on forced labor.

The invocation was historically significant, but its practical limitations were equally significant. The ILO does not possess enforcement authority over the junta's military operations. Moreover, the Article 33 resolution's call for business disengagement from military-linked enterprises creates a risk of collateral harm to civilian workers if it is implemented without careful distinctions.

The International Court of Justice case concerning the genocide of the Rohingya, which entered a new phase of hearings in January 2026, may represent the most durable accountability mechanism currently underway. However, judicial proceedings move slowly, and civilians facing military airstrikes cannot wait for an eventual ICJ judgment.

Existing frameworks share a structural bias toward engaging the de facto power, the junta, rather than the political authority viewed as legitimate by much of the population, the NUG. This bias is partly practical because the junta controls weapons, major cities, and sections of the country's territory.

However, it has also proved self-defeating. Four years of engagement have not moderated the junta's conduct. Airstrikes against civilian targets continued on the same day ASEAN issued its Joint Communique in July 2025. Meanwhile, the NUG, which commands widespread public loyalty and maintains influence across much of the country's territory, has been systematically excluded from the international frameworks intended to resolve a conflict to which it is a central party.

V. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following five recommendations are directed toward ASEAN member states, UN bodies, and governments with diplomatic or economic leverage over the parties to Myanmar's conflict. They are not intended to replace existing frameworks. Rather, they seek to address the specific structural failures that have made those frameworks ineffective.

1. Formally Engage the NUG as a Legitimate Party in International Processes

The exclusion of the NUG from ASEAN, UN, and bilateral diplomatic processes has been one of the most significant structural failures of the international response. A government recognized by 93 percent of the population it claims to represent, and which maintains influence across much of the country's territory, cannot be treated as a peripheral actor in frameworks intended to resolve the national crisis.

ASEAN member states should take the following actions:

  1. AFormalize NUG participation in ASEAN processes concerning Myanmar, treating the April 2025 virtual meeting with the ASEAN Chair as a precedent rather than an exception.
  2. BEstablish a clear distinction between the junta as a de facto military and security actor and the NUG as the political authority recognized by the civilian population.
  3. CCondition any normalization of the junta's diplomatic status, including Min Aung Hlaing's efforts to restore ASEAN engagement following his April 2026 inauguration, on measurable progress toward political dialogue with the NUG. Normalization should not be granted in response to performative gestures such as Suu Kyi's transfer to house arrest.

The UN General Assembly should also expand the mandate of the UN Special Envoy for Myanmar to include direct and sustained engagement with the NUG. The current practice of treating the junta and the NUG as symmetrically legitimate actors should be abandoned.

2. Separate Humanitarian Access from Political Recognition

One of the primary obstacles to humanitarian relief in Myanmar is that many aid channels pass through, and are therefore controlled by, the junta. The junta's weaponization of humanitarian access, most clearly demonstrated during the earthquake response, is not an isolated incident. It is a deliberate political and military tactic.

The international humanitarian community should take the following actions:

  1. ASubstantially expand funding and operational support for cross-border and non-junta humanitarian channels, including organizations operating through NUG-aligned civil society networks and the administrative structures of ethnic armed organizations.
  2. BEstablish a Myanmar Humanitarian Access Fund, jointly administered by the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs and regional civil society networks. The fund should support local responders capable of operating in areas that international agencies cannot safely or independently access.
  3. CReject any arrangement that makes humanitarian access exclusively dependent on junta cooperation. The 15 percent funding rate for the 2025 humanitarian response was partly the result of donor risk aversion toward supporting organizations operating outside junta-controlled channels.

The withdrawal of the United States from international aid programs has significantly reduced funding available for Myanmar's humanitarian response. European Union member states and regional donors, including Japan, South Korea, and Australia, should urgently work to fill this gap.

3. Reject and Isolate the Junta's Manufactured Legitimacy

ANFREL, International IDEA, Human Rights Watch, the UN Special Rapporteur, and numerous independent observers concluded that the elections held from December 2025 through January 2026 failed to meet internationally recognized standards for genuine democratic elections.

The international response to Min Aung Hlaing's April 2026 inauguration as president will determine whether the junta's strategy of manufacturing political legitimacy succeeds.

Governments should take the following actions:

  1. AFormally declare that Min Aung Hlaing's government is not recognized as the product of a legitimate electoral process. This position should be reflected in bilateral diplomatic correspondence and multilateral statements.
  2. BMaintain and expand targeted sanctions against individuals and entities directly responsible for, or materially supporting, atrocity crimes. These measures should include arms suppliers and commercial enterprises linked to the junta, while explicitly protecting legitimate civilian economic activity from sanctions exposure.
  3. CCoordinate a joint statement among democratic governments, including the European Union, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, Australia, and Canada. The statement should identify specific benchmarks that must be met before any normalization of diplomatic relations is considered. The junta should not be permitted to determine the conditions of its own political rehabilitation.

Suu Kyi's transfer to house arrest should not be treated as equivalent to the unconditional release of the approximately 30,000 political prisoners who remain in detention.

4. Strengthen Accountability Mechanisms for Atrocity Crimes

The Myanmar military has committed documented war crimes and crimes against humanity throughout the conflict. These acts include deliberate airstrikes against schools, hospitals, and displacement camps; the forced conscription of Rohingya civilians; and the killing of at least one union leader during a wage protest.

These violations have occurred despite the existence of international frameworks intended to prevent such crimes. The persistent gap between documentation and accountability has become one of the defining features of the international community's failure.

Concrete steps should include the following:

  1. AAccelerate support for the ICJ genocide case and the Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar. This support should include adequate funding and measures to ensure that collected evidence can be used in future criminal prosecutions.
  2. BSupport the use of universal jurisdiction in the domestic courts of third countries. This should build on the precedent established by Argentinian arrest warrants issued in connection with atrocities against the Rohingya, particularly those targeting junta commanders.
  3. CEstablish a dedicated Myanmar Atrocity Documentation Fund administered by the UN Human Rights Council. This fund should ensure that community-level evidence collection does not depend on the political priorities or continued participation of any single donor.

Accountability is not separate from conflict resolution. It is a necessary condition for any political settlement that does not simply reward the party with superior military power.

5. Address the Structural Conditions Sustaining the Junta's External Support

China is the junta's most consequential external supporter. It provides diplomatic protection at the UN Security Council, supplies arms and dual-use technology to the Tatmadaw, and maintains economic lifelines through cross-border trade.

India, despite its stated neutrality, has maintained commercial and strategic relations with the junta. Russia continues to supply military equipment. Without pressure on these relationships, the junta has little material incentive to change its conduct.

This recommendation must therefore be directed toward governments that possess leverage over these bilateral relationships.

  1. AThe European Union and the United States should expand secondary sanctions against companies that supply arms or dual-use technology to Myanmar's military. These measures should directly target the Chinese and Russian supply chains that sustain the Tatmadaw's air campaign.
  2. BASEAN governments with influence over China, particularly Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines, should communicate directly with Beijing that continued military support for a regime committing atrocity crimes is an obstacle to ASEAN-China relations, not merely a bilateral issue between China and Myanmar.
  3. CIndia should be engaged on the specific risks that prolonged junta rule in Myanmar poses to security along India's northeastern border and to its broader interest in a stable ASEAN region. This reframing would align India's strategic interests with civilian protection rather than place them in opposition to it.

No external pressure mechanism will be sufficient by itself. However, the junta's survival has depended significantly on external support that has imposed few serious costs on its providers. That must change if the structural dynamics of the conflict are to shift.

VI. CONCLUSION

Myanmar's civil war is not intractable because the interests of all parties are inherently irreconcilable. The overwhelming majority of Myanmar's population, as demonstrated by the 2020 election, four years of armed resistance, and consistent survey data, does not support military rule.

Despite its limitations and the challenges of operating largely in exile, the NUG represents a genuine political alternative with substantial popular legitimacy. The ethnic armed organizations that control much of the country's territory share a fundamental interest in establishing a federal democratic system that protects minority rights. Such a goal is structurally incompatible with continued junta rule, but it may be achievable through a civilian-led political transition.

What makes Myanmar's conflict appear intractable is not the absence of a viable political outcome. It is the international community's systematic failure to support the conditions necessary for that outcome to emerge.

ASEAN has engaged with the junta while marginalizing the civilian government. The UN Security Council has been obstructed by the junta's great-power supporters. Humanitarian frameworks have been compromised by the junta's control over access. A series of performative gestures, including Suu Kyi's transfer to house arrest, elections conducted amid active airstrikes, and ceasefires that lasted only a few weeks, have repeatedly been allowed to substitute for substantive progress.

The ILO's invocation of Article 33, the ICJ genocide proceedings, and the April 2025 meeting between ASEAN and the NUG each represent partial steps in the right direction. However, neither individually nor collectively do they constitute a comprehensive strategy.

A genuine strategy would require accepting that the junta cannot remain the primary interlocutor in efforts to resolve Myanmar's crisis. It would require placing the NUG and civil society at the center of any viable political process. It would also require recognizing that the international community's credibility in Myanmar depends on whether its stated commitments to civilian protection and democratic governance are matched by meaningful action when a military government openly defies both.

The cost of the current approach is being paid by the 22 million people who require humanitarian assistance, the 5.2 million people who have been displaced, the union leaders killed during wage protests, and the communities attempting to rebuild after an earthquake while the government that requested international assistance continues to bomb them.

That cost must shape the international community's assessment of what it is willing to do differently.

V. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following five recommendations are directed toward ASEAN member states, UN bodies, and governments with diplomatic or economic leverage over the parties to Myanmar's conflict. They are not intended to replace existing frameworks. Rather, they seek to address the specific structural failures that have made those frameworks ineffective.

1. Formally Engage the NUG as a Legitimate Party in International Processes

The exclusion of the NUG from ASEAN, UN, and bilateral diplomatic processes has been one of the most significant structural failures of the international response. A government recognized by 93 percent of the population it claims to represent, and which maintains influence across much of the country's territory, cannot be treated as a peripheral actor in frameworks intended to resolve the national crisis.

ASEAN member states should take the following actions:

  1. AFormalize NUG participation in ASEAN processes concerning Myanmar, treating the April 2025 virtual meeting with the ASEAN Chair as a precedent rather than an exception.
  2. BEstablish a clear distinction between the junta as a de facto military and security actor and the NUG as the political authority recognized by the civilian population.
  3. CCondition any normalization of the junta's diplomatic status, including Min Aung Hlaing's efforts to restore ASEAN engagement following his April 2026 inauguration, on measurable progress toward political dialogue with the NUG. Normalization should not be granted in response to performative gestures such as Suu Kyi's transfer to house arrest.

The UN General Assembly should also expand the mandate of the UN Special Envoy for Myanmar to include direct and sustained engagement with the NUG. The current practice of treating the junta and the NUG as symmetrically legitimate actors should be abandoned.

2. Separate Humanitarian Access from Political Recognition

One of the primary obstacles to humanitarian relief in Myanmar is that many aid channels pass through, and are therefore controlled by, the junta. The junta's weaponization of humanitarian access, most clearly demonstrated during the earthquake response, is not an isolated incident. It is a deliberate political and military tactic.

The international humanitarian community should take the following actions:

  1. ASubstantially expand funding and operational support for cross-border and non-junta humanitarian channels, including organizations operating through NUG-aligned civil society networks and the administrative structures of ethnic armed organizations.
  2. BEstablish a Myanmar Humanitarian Access Fund, jointly administered by the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs and regional civil society networks. The fund should support local responders capable of operating in areas that international agencies cannot safely or independently access.
  3. CReject any arrangement that makes humanitarian access exclusively dependent on junta cooperation. The 15 percent funding rate for the 2025 humanitarian response was partly the result of donor risk aversion toward supporting organizations operating outside junta-controlled channels.

The withdrawal of the United States from international aid programs has significantly reduced funding available for Myanmar's humanitarian response. European Union member states and regional donors, including Japan, South Korea, and Australia, should urgently work to fill this gap.

3. Reject and Isolate the Junta's Manufactured Legitimacy

ANFREL, International IDEA, Human Rights Watch, the UN Special Rapporteur, and numerous independent observers concluded that the elections held from December 2025 through January 2026 failed to meet internationally recognized standards for genuine democratic elections.

The international response to Min Aung Hlaing's April 2026 inauguration as president will determine whether the junta's strategy of manufacturing political legitimacy succeeds.

Governments should take the following actions:

  1. AFormally declare that Min Aung Hlaing's government is not recognized as the product of a legitimate electoral process. This position should be reflected in bilateral diplomatic correspondence and multilateral statements.
  2. BMaintain and expand targeted sanctions against individuals and entities directly responsible for, or materially supporting, atrocity crimes. These measures should include arms suppliers and commercial enterprises linked to the junta, while explicitly protecting legitimate civilian economic activity from sanctions exposure.
  3. CCoordinate a joint statement among democratic governments, including the European Union, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, Australia, and Canada. The statement should identify specific benchmarks that must be met before any normalization of diplomatic relations is considered. The junta should not be permitted to determine the conditions of its own political rehabilitation.

Suu Kyi's transfer to house arrest should not be treated as equivalent to the unconditional release of the approximately 30,000 political prisoners who remain in detention.

4. Strengthen Accountability Mechanisms for Atrocity Crimes

The Myanmar military has committed documented war crimes and crimes against humanity throughout the conflict. These acts include deliberate airstrikes against schools, hospitals, and displacement camps; the forced conscription of Rohingya civilians; and the killing of at least one union leader during a wage protest.

These violations have occurred despite the existence of international frameworks intended to prevent such crimes. The persistent gap between documentation and accountability has become one of the defining features of the international community's failure.

Concrete steps should include the following:

  1. AAccelerate support for the ICJ genocide case and the Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar. This support should include adequate funding and measures to ensure that collected evidence can be used in future criminal prosecutions.
  2. BSupport the use of universal jurisdiction in the domestic courts of third countries. This should build on the precedent established by Argentinian arrest warrants issued in connection with atrocities against the Rohingya, particularly those targeting junta commanders.
  3. CEstablish a dedicated Myanmar Atrocity Documentation Fund administered by the UN Human Rights Council. This fund should ensure that community-level evidence collection does not depend on the political priorities or continued participation of any single donor.

Accountability is not separate from conflict resolution. It is a necessary condition for any political settlement that does not simply reward the party with superior military power.

5. Address the Structural Conditions Sustaining the Junta's External Support

China is the junta's most consequential external supporter. It provides diplomatic protection at the UN Security Council, supplies arms and dual-use technology to the Tatmadaw, and maintains economic lifelines through cross-border trade.

India, despite its stated neutrality, has maintained commercial and strategic relations with the junta. Russia continues to supply military equipment. Without pressure on these relationships, the junta has little material incentive to change its conduct.

This recommendation must therefore be directed toward governments that possess leverage over these bilateral relationships.

  1. AThe European Union and the United States should expand secondary sanctions against companies that supply arms or dual-use technology to Myanmar's military. These measures should directly target the Chinese and Russian supply chains that sustain the Tatmadaw's air campaign.
  2. BASEAN governments with influence over China, particularly Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines, should communicate directly with Beijing that continued military support for a regime committing atrocity crimes is an obstacle to ASEAN-China relations, not merely a bilateral issue between China and Myanmar.
  3. CIndia should be engaged on the specific risks that prolonged junta rule in Myanmar poses to security along India's northeastern border and to its broader interest in a stable ASEAN region. This reframing would align India's strategic interests with civilian protection rather than place them in opposition to it.

No external pressure mechanism will be sufficient by itself. However, the junta's survival has depended significantly on external support that has imposed few serious costs on its providers. That must change if the structural dynamics of the conflict are to shift.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Across Southeast Asia, millions of people live without citizenship in the countries where they were born, raised, and continue to reside. They are not recent arrivals or temporary migrants. Many can trace their presence in these countries across multiple generations. Yet under the legal frameworks of Myanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia, they are not recognized as citizens. Without citizenship, they have no enforceable claim to healthcare, education, formal employment, or equal protection under the law.

The philosopher Hannah Arendt described citizenship as "the right to have rights." Statelessness, then, is the condition of possessing none of them. A stateless person may be unable to open a bank account, obtain a passport, register a marriage, enroll a child in public school, or access state-subsidized medical care. Stateless individuals can be detained, exploited, and deported, yet they often have no legal standing from which to object.

This brief examines three distinct but interconnected cases: the Rohingya of Myanmar, whose statelessness was codified through the 1982 Citizenship Law; the hill tribe communities of northern Thailand, including the Karen, Akha, Lahu, Hmong, and others, whose exclusion from citizenship can be traced directly to colonial borders they had no role in drawing; and the communities of Sabah in East Malaysia, including the Bajau Laut and descendants of cross-border populations whose presence predates the states that now exclude them.

In each case, the mechanism of exclusion differs. The consequence is the same: invisibility before the law, vulnerability in everyday life, and the intergenerational transmission of a legal status that individuals cannot easily escape.

The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) confirmed that 4.4 million people worldwide were stateless at the end of 2024, with the majority concentrated in Asia. The agency's ten-year #IBelong Campaign, which ran from 2014 to 2024, produced meaningful but incomplete results. During the campaign, 613,100 stateless individuals acquired nationality. However, the launch of the Global Alliance to End Statelessness in October 2024 reflected the international community's recognition that the problem remains far from resolved.

This brief recommends five interventions targeting the legal, administrative, and political obstacles that have sustained statelessness across all three countries.

I. THE STRUCTURE OF STATELESSNESS: COLONIAL BORDERS AND THE CITIZENSHIP TRAP

Understanding why statelessness remains so persistent in Southeast Asia requires an examination of its origins. The borders of Myanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia were not drawn by the communities living within them. They were largely established by colonial powers, particularly the British, through a series of treaties and territorial demarcations during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

These borders divided ethnic communities, mountain populations, and coastal societies across national boundaries they did not choose and, in many cases, did not know existed.

The Global Citizenship Observatory (GLOBALCIT) at the European University Institute has documented the direct consequences of this process: "Many state-building projects were confined to borders arbitrarily drawn by colonial powers and have since witnessed a dynamic process of re-formulating citizenship regimes, often nurtured by inter-communal tensions that had been exacerbated during colonial times."

The Karen people were divided across what later became the Myanmar-Thailand border. The Akha, whose traditional territories span areas that are now part of southern China, Myanmar, Laos, and northern Thailand, found themselves subjected to the citizenship systems of states whose formation occurred long after their communities had settled on the land.

Similarly, the Bajau Laut of Sabah traditionally occupied maritime territories that colonial boundary-making divided among Malaysia, the Philippines, and Indonesia. These new borders left many Bajau Laut without a clearly recognized national home in any of the three countries.

After independence, governments throughout the region inherited these borders. In many cases, they also used citizenship laws as instruments of ethnic consolidation. National communities were legally defined in ways that excluded populations considered too culturally distinct, economically marginal, or politically threatening to include. In the case of the Rohingya, citizenship exclusion became part of a broader campaign of political and ethnic persecution.

As a result, statelessness in Southeast Asia is not primarily the product of migration or administrative error. It is, to a significant extent, the product of deliberate legal design that has been maintained for decades.

A 2025 issue of the Melbourne Asia Review, examining the Cambridge University Press volume Statelessness in Asia, describes statelessness as "deeply intertwined with colonial legacies, state formation, migration, ethnic nationalism, gender discrimination, and restrictive citizenship laws." It further explains that the issue "demonstrates both the persistence of historical exclusion and the emergence of new patterns of displacement and nationality insecurity."

A serious analysis of statelessness must therefore consider both dimensions: its historical origins in colonial boundary-making and the contemporary legal structures through which states continue to sustain exclusion.

II. MYANMAR: STATELESSNESS AS STATE POLICY, THE ROHINGYA AND THE 1982 CITIZENSHIP LAW

The Rohingya are an ethnically and linguistically distinct Muslim population with a documented presence in the Arakan region of present-day Myanmar that predates 1823, the threshold year used by Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law to determine indigenous status.

Despite this history, the law does not include the Rohingya among the country's 135 officially recognized national races. Under the law, citizenship is divided into three categories: full citizenship, associate citizenship, and naturalized citizenship. However, access to all three categories depends either on membership in a recognized national race or on documentary proof that a person's ancestors lived in Myanmar before 1823.

Most Rohingya families cannot realistically provide such documentation, particularly given the limited and inconsistent recordkeeping systems of the colonial period.

The 1982 law did not emerge in isolation. A 1974 citizenship law had already begun restricting the Rohingya's legal status. In 1978, a military operation known as "Naga Min" expelled more than 200,000 Rohingya from Myanmar, although most were later permitted to return.

The 1982 Citizenship Law formalized their exclusion in a way that made statelessness self-perpetuating. Citizenship generally requires at least one citizen parent, but Rohingya parents are excluded from citizenship from the outset. Each new generation is therefore born stateless because the previous generation was also stateless.

The practical consequences have been extensively documented. Under the 1982 legal framework, Rohingya communities face restrictions on freedom of movement, denial of access to higher education, exclusion from public office, and significant barriers to registering marriages and births through official channels.

When births are not officially registered, children enter the world without documentation and with no practical legal pathway out of statelessness. These restrictions did not prevent the Rohingya from living in Myanmar. Instead, they created conditions in which violence against them could occur without meaningful legal protection or institutional accountability.

The military campaign launched against the Rohingya in August 2017 forced more than 700,000 people to cross the border into Bangladesh within a matter of weeks. Today, more than 1.6 million Rohingya live in Bangladesh, representing the largest concentration of stateless people in any single country.

Those who remain in Myanmar continue to face severe restrictions, forced military conscription by both the junta and the Arakan Army, and some of the country's worst humanitarian conditions.

Hearings on the merits of The Gambia's genocide case against Myanmar at the International Court of Justice (ICJ) began on January 12, 2026, with a ruling expected before the end of the year. As the American Bar Association's International Law News program noted in September 2025, statelessness functioned as a legal precondition for identity-based destruction. It erased the Rohingya's legal recognition and social visibility before widespread physical violence began.

The ongoing civil war has not improved the Rohingya's position. Both the junta and the Arakan Army have reportedly used Rohingya civilians as human shields and forced combatants. The Arakan Army now controls most of the northern townships in Rakhine State, but it has not extended citizenship rights or meaningful legal protection to Rohingya communities.

Repatriation to Myanmar is not currently viable. No credible pathway to citizenship exists, and no political or military authority capable of guaranteeing the physical safety of returning Rohingya has demonstrated a willingness to do so.

III. THAILAND: COLONIAL BORDERS AND THE HILL TRIBE CITIZENSHIP DEFICIT

Thailand's hill tribe communities include the Karen, Akha, Lahu, Hmong, Lisu, Mien, Htin, Khamu, and other ethnic groups. These communities occupy the northern highlands, a region where colonial-era borders divided populations across what later became Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, and China.

Many of these groups had lived continuously in the upland regions of present-day northern Thailand for generations before the Thai state fully incorporated their territories.

A 1972 amendment to Thailand's Nationality Act tightened citizenship restrictions in response to migration from Myanmar. It created a legal framework under which communities with longstanding territorial ties to Thailand could nevertheless be classified as foreigners or denied recognition as legal persons.

Before the Thai government's landmark Cabinet Resolution in October 2024, the country's registered stateless population stood at nearly 600,000. Approximately 130,000 people, nearly one-quarter of the total, lived in Chiang Mai Province. Most were members of indigenous ethnic minorities from mountainous border regions.

The Thai state's historical portrayal of these communities further intensified their legal vulnerability. Beginning in the 1960s, national security discourse often characterized hill tribe communities as internal threats. They were portrayed as culturally incompatible with dominant ideas of "Thainess," connected to communist insurgent movements, or associated with opium cultivation and land-use practices considered inconsistent with state sovereignty.

The collective classification of nine ethnic groups under the term "chao khao" also reinforced this process of exclusion. The term and the language surrounding it positioned these communities as outsiders, helping create the social and political conditions under which citizenship denial could be justified.

Without citizenship, hill tribe members face a wide range of documented restrictions. They may be unable to travel freely between provinces without special permission. They cannot obtain passports or legally travel outside Thailand. They are excluded from formal employment in many sectors and face barriers to state healthcare and education programs. They may also be unable to register property, businesses, or legal transactions.

An Akha elder born in Chiang Mai Province fifty years ago may be unable to prove that she has ever lived anywhere else. She has no other country to which she belongs, yet she remains stateless.

Thailand has taken significant steps toward reform. In October 2024, the Cabinet approved an accelerated pathway to permanent residency and Thai nationality for approximately 484,000 of the country's nearly 600,000 registered stateless residents. The policy covered around 335,000 long-term residents eligible for permanent residency and more than 142,000 of their Thailand-born children who were eligible for nationality.

UNHCR described the resolution as potentially "the most dramatic reduction of statelessness the world has ever seen."

By December 2025, Thai authorities had resolved more than 100,000 cases under the policy, and over 12,000 people had acquired Thai nationality. The implementation of the resolution represents a genuine milestone.

However, its long-term success depends on consistent administrative implementation at the provincial and district levels. An estimated 100,000 or more stateless people who are not covered by the October 2024 resolution still lack a clear pathway to citizenship.

More than 100,000 stateless people had already acquired Thai citizenship between 2008 and 2024. If fully implemented, the October 2024 resolution could become a major turning point. However, the history of statelessness policy in Thailand cautions against declaring the problem resolved too early.

Administrative barriers, documentation gaps, local discretion, and the geographic isolation of many affected communities have historically transformed official promises into prolonged application backlogs.

IV. MALAYSIA: SABAH AND THE INTERGENERATIONAL CITIZENSHIP DEFICIT

Malaysia's stateless population includes descendants of Tamil Indians brought to the Malay Peninsula as plantation workers during the colonial period, abandoned and foundling children, individuals affected by gender-discriminatory nationality laws, and communities in Sabah, East Malaysia.

The situation in Sabah most directly parallels that of the hill tribe communities in northern Thailand.

Malaysia reported 120,587 stateless people to UNHCR in 2024, but this number is widely understood to underestimate the true scale of the problem.

According to the Institute on Statelessness and Inclusion (ISI) and the Statelessness Encyclopedia for Asia Pacific (SEAP), the Chief Minister of Sabah announced in March 2022 that 810,443 Sabah residents were non-citizens. This number was nearly twice as high as an earlier UNHCR estimate of approximately 450,000 stateless people in Sabah alone.

The ISI's 2024 joint submission to the Committee on the Rights of the Child, which drew on data from the Department of Statistics Malaysia, found that more than 27 percent of Sabah's population of 3.742 million consisted of non-citizens. This amounted to approximately 1,043,400 people, including around 371,100 children between the ages of 0 and 19.

Sabah's non-citizen population includes several overlapping groups. These include the Bajau Laut, a seafaring and coastal population whose traditional territories span the waters of present-day Malaysia, the Philippines, and Indonesia; descendants of migrants from the southern Philippines and Indonesia who settled in Sabah across multiple generations; and indigenous Orang Asal communities that were present before the formation of Malaysia in 1963 but were never granted complete citizenship documentation.

The primary mechanism of exclusion is documentary. Many of these populations have lived in Sabah for generations but lack the official records required under Malaysia's citizenship system, including birth certificates, family lineage records, and ancestral registration documents.

They have no clear pathway to citizenship in Malaysia. At the same time, they often have no viable claim to citizenship in the Philippines or Indonesia. No state accepts responsibility for them.

These communities are stateless not because they were formally expelled from a country, but because the legal systems of multiple states have collectively failed to include them.

For indigenous Orang Asal communities, the situation is particularly contradictory. Malaysia's Federal Constitution includes provisions intended to protect indigenous rights, and Orang Asal communities are legally recognized as Bumiputera. The term, meaning "sons of the soil," refers to a category that receives preferential status under Malaysian law.

However, many Orang Asal residents of Sabah possess only red identification cards, which indicate permanent residency, rather than the blue MyKad, which confirms citizenship. Their Bumiputera status exists in law, but their citizenship is not recognized in practice.

In October 2024, Malaysia's lower house of Parliament passed the Constitution (Amendment) Bill 2024 by a near-unanimous vote of 206 to 1.

The bill included a genuinely progressive reform by granting Malaysian women the same right as Malaysian men to automatically transmit citizenship to children born abroad. This change addressed a longstanding form of gender discrimination in Malaysia's nationality law.

However, the bill also removed automatic citizenship for children born in Malaysia to at least one parent who was a permanent resident. This provision had been the primary legal pathway through which stateless indigenous children could eventually regularize their status.

The United Nations Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR), SUHAKAM, the Human Rights Commission of Malaysia, the ISI, and numerous civil society organizations raised serious objections to this change. They warned that it would remove an essential safeguard against childhood statelessness and further entrench intergenerational exclusion.

V. THE SHARED CONSEQUENCE: WHAT STATELESSNESS ACTUALLY MEANS

The three cases differ in their historical origins and legal mechanisms. However, they share the same lived consequences.

Without citizenship documents, stateless individuals may be unable to enroll in state schools. In some cases, they can enroll only in limited and underfunded programs that provide little legal protection or opportunity for advancement.

They may be unable to access state-subsidized healthcare and are often confined to informal, unregulated labor markets where exploitation is structurally enabled and rarely challenged through legal channels. Formal employment in most sectors requires documents that stateless individuals do not possess.

Stateless people may also be unable to open bank accounts, obtain driver's licenses, or register telecommunications services in their own names. They may be unable to register marriages, which can prevent the official registration of their children and continue the cycle of statelessness.

They cannot obtain passports or legally travel between countries. UNHCR has documented that stateless people face "a lifetime of obstacles without access to education, healthcare, employment, and freedom of movement."

Writing in the Harvard International Review in January 2021, Florian Bochert described stateless laborers as a "pawn sacrifice of the global labor regime." Their legal invisibility makes informal and exploitative labor not an accidental outcome, but a predictable consequence of exclusion from legal personhood.

The consequences are also shaped by gender. Women in stateless communities face overlapping vulnerabilities, including gender-discriminatory nationality laws, denial of reproductive healthcare, barriers to registering marriages and births, and heightened exposure to human trafficking.

Interaction with government authorities may itself carry the risk of detention. As a result, stateless women often lack meaningful protection from law enforcement and may be unable to report abuse or exploitation safely.

The intergenerational dimension is the most consequential. Statelessness is rarely resolved through individual effort alone.

A child born to stateless parents in northern Thailand, Rakhine State, or Sabah inherits not only the economic disadvantages of the parents but also their legal nonexistence. In many communities, three or four generations have passed without a single family member receiving citizenship documentation.

The accumulation of disadvantage across generations in education, health, employment, political representation, and legal protection is fundamentally different from poverty alone. It is the systematic exclusion of entire communities from the legal status that makes claims against the state possible.

VI. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following five recommendations are directed toward the governments of Myanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia; the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN); UNHCR; and bilateral development partners operating in the region.

They are designed to address both the immediate administrative barriers preventing access to citizenship and the broader legal structures that allow statelessness to continue across generations.

1. Repeal or Fundamentally Reform Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law

The 1982 Citizenship Law is the legal foundation of Rohingya statelessness. It cannot be corrected through minor administrative reforms because its central structure is itself discriminatory. Citizenship is conditioned on membership in an officially recognized national race. This is the source of the problem, not merely a failure of implementation.

The National Unity Government's Federal Democracy Charter explicitly recognizes Rohingya rights. However, this recognition must be translated into concrete legal commitments.

Any future political transition in Myanmar must make the repeal of the 1982 Citizenship Law and its replacement with a citizenship system based on residence, birth, and descent a foundational requirement. Such a framework must comply with international human rights standards and cannot be treated as an aspirational goal to be addressed only after other political priorities.

In the immediate term:

  1. AThe NUG should formally adopt and publicly release a citizenship roadmap for the Rohingya. This roadmap should specify the legal reforms it would implement during a political transition and demonstrate that its human rights commitments extend to the population most severely excluded under the current system.
  2. BInternational donors and development partners that condition support on governance reforms should explicitly identify the repeal of the 1982 Citizenship Law as a measurable benchmark.
  3. CThe ICJ genocide proceedings should be supported with comprehensive documentation demonstrating how statelessness functioned as a legal precondition for the atrocities committed in 2017. This would help establish, as a matter of international law, that legislated statelessness can serve as a preparatory mechanism for genocide.

2. Establish a Regional Documentation and Birth Registration Framework

Across all three countries, the primary administrative barrier to citizenship is the lack of documentation.

Many stateless individuals cannot provide birth records, family lineage documents, or other evidence required by citizenship application procedures. This is not necessarily because they lack a legitimate claim to citizenship. Rather, documentation systems were never designed to reach their communities.

Hill tribe villages in northern Thailand, remote coastal communities in Sabah, and displaced Rohingya families all share a common problem. Their long-term presence has never been officially recorded in a form that citizenship authorities can easily process.

ASEAN, in partnership with UNHCR and the ISI, should develop a regional mobile documentation framework that brings registration services directly to affected communities rather than requiring residents to navigate distant and complex central bureaucracies.

Specific measures should include the following:

  1. AThailand's October 2024 Cabinet Resolution should be evaluated regularly based on measurable citizenship outcomes. Dedicated funding should support mobile registration units operating in Chiang Mai, Chiang Rai, and Mae Hong Son provinces, where stateless populations are concentrated.
  2. BMalaysia should establish a dedicated Sabah Documentation Taskforce responsible for registering the citizenship claims of long-term indigenous and cross-border communities within a clearly defined period. Civil society organizations should participate in monitoring the process.
  3. CMyanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia should commit to universal birth registration. Every child born within their territories should receive a birth certificate regardless of the citizenship status of the parents. This would provide a minimum safeguard against the intergenerational transmission of statelessness.

3. Decouple Access to Basic Services from Citizenship Documentation

Citizenship reform is a long-term objective, but stateless communities require immediate access to essential services. They should not be denied medical treatment or education while administrative and political processes continue.

The connection between citizenship documents and access to healthcare, education, and emergency assistance is not legally inevitable. It is a policy choice. Governments can and do provide public services to non-citizens.

Thailand's partial extension of healthcare to stateless hill tribe communities and Malaysia's limited educational programs for non-citizen children in Sabah provide important precedents that can be expanded.

Governments should take the following actions:

  1. AExplicitly guarantee access to primary and emergency healthcare for all residents, regardless of documentation status. Funding should be provided through dedicated national health budget allocations.
  2. BGuarantee unconditional access to public primary education for every child physically present in the country. Community-language instruction should be available in regions with large stateless populations.
  3. CEnsure that stateless individuals can report crimes, access courts, and communicate with law enforcement without facing detention solely because of their documentation status.

This protection is necessary to prevent stateless communities from becoming captive labor pools for employers who know that workers cannot safely seek legal assistance.

4. Reform Nationality Laws to Eliminate Gender Discrimination and Intergenerational Traps

Malaysia's October 2024 constitutional amendments granted women equal rights to transmit citizenship to children born abroad. This was a significant reform. However, the same legislation removed automatic citizenship for children born in Malaysia to permanent resident parents, creating new risks of intergenerational statelessness while addressing an existing form of discrimination.

In Thailand, the complexity of nationality law can create situations in which children born to one stateless parent and one citizen parent still encounter serious documentation barriers.

In Myanmar, the race-based citizenship framework makes it nearly impossible for Rohingya individuals to obtain citizenship regardless of their parents' documentation.

Specific reforms should include the following:

  1. AMalaysia should ensure that the progressive provisions of the 2024 amendments are applied retroactively. It should also establish alternative citizenship pathways for children of permanent residents who lost their automatic entitlement, particularly children from indigenous Orang Asal communities and long-term stateless families with no other legal options.
  2. BThailand should simplify its nationality laws and establish clear, accessible pathways to citizenship for second-generation and third-generation residents, regardless of their parents' documentation status. It should fully implement the October 2024 Cabinet Resolution and allow independent verification of its outcomes.
  3. CASEAN should formally encourage member states to join the 1954 Convention Relating to the Status of Stateless Persons and the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness.

Most major states in Southeast Asia have not ratified either convention, significantly limiting the reach of international legal protections for stateless populations.

5. Create Accountability Mechanisms for Statelessness Reduction

UNHCR's #IBelong Campaign established the goal of ending statelessness by 2024. Thailand pledged to achieve "zero statelessness," while Malaysia committed to reducing its backlog of citizenship applications.

However, these commitments were not connected to binding accountability mechanisms, independent verification, or consequences for noncompliance.

As a result, public commitments to reform have repeatedly advanced more quickly than actual implementation.

Thailand's October 2024 Cabinet Resolution is unprecedented in scale. However, its December 2025 progress report, which documented 100,000 resolved cases from a registered population of nearly 600,000 stateless people, shows that policy ambition and administrative implementation remain separate challenges.

ASEAN should establish a Regional Statelessness Monitoring Mechanism within the ASEAN Intergovernmental Commission on Human Rights (AICHR).

The mechanism should:

  1. ARequire annual reports from member states on stateless population estimates, citizenship application processing times, application outcomes, and documentation initiatives.
  2. BCommission independent verification of government figures by UNHCR and qualified civil society organizations.
  3. CPublish verified progress data so that bilateral partners, international institutions, and the public can assess government performance.

Development partners, including the European Union, Japan, Australia, and Canada, should connect governance assistance and trade preferences to measurable and independently verified reductions in stateless populations rather than relying solely on legislative commitments.

The persistent gap between commitment and implementation in all three countries will not close without external accountability and meaningful consequences for inaction.

VII. CONCLUSION

Statelessness in Myanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia is not merely a historical problem awaiting a technical solution. It is a continuing policy condition in which governments have repeatedly maintained legal frameworks that exclude millions of people from citizenship and, consequently, from healthcare, education, formal employment, and protection from violence.

The origins of this exclusion lie partly in colonial borders that divided communities among states they had no role in creating. However, the continuation of that exclusion is a contemporary political choice.

Myanmar enacted the 1982 Citizenship Law and maintained it under both military and civilian governments.

Thailand preserved nationality procedures so complex and inaccessible that hundreds of thousands of people with multigenerational ties to the country remained stateless despite decades of government commitments. Although the October 2024 Cabinet Resolution represents a meaningful change, implementation remains incomplete.

Malaysia adopted constitutional amendments in October 2024 that advanced gender equality in citizenship law while simultaneously removing automatic citizenship from the children of permanent residents. This change left indigenous Orang Asal communities and long-established stateless families more vulnerable.

Hannah Arendt's formulation captures what is at stake: citizenship is the right to have rights.

A stateless person is not simply poor or socially marginalized. Before the law, that person may have no recognized standing. Stateless individuals may be unable to make legal claims, obtain protection, or pass recognized legal status to their children.

Consider a Rohingya grandmother living in a displacement camp in Bangladesh, an Akha elder in Chiang Mai who received a nationality card only after four decades of advocacy, or a Bajau Laut child in Sabah born to parents who are citizens of no country.

These are not exceptional cases. They are predictable outcomes of legal systems designed to exclude.

The launch of UNHCR's Global Alliance to End Statelessness in October 2024 reflected the international community's recognition that the goals of the ten-year #IBelong Campaign had not been fully achieved.

The remaining gap is not primarily technical. It is political.

States already possess the legal tools required to resolve statelessness. What has been missing in the three cases examined in this brief is the political willingness to use those tools against the ethnic, territorial, and economic interests that have sustained exclusion.

The international community must therefore change the political cost calculation. The diplomatic and economic consequences of continuing statelessness must become greater than the perceived costs of resolving it.

VI. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following five recommendations are directed toward the governments of Myanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia; the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN); UNHCR; and bilateral development partners operating in the region.

They are designed to address both the immediate administrative barriers preventing access to citizenship and the broader legal structures that allow statelessness to continue across generations.

1. Repeal or Fundamentally Reform Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law

The 1982 Citizenship Law is the legal foundation of Rohingya statelessness. It cannot be corrected through minor administrative reforms because its central structure is itself discriminatory. Citizenship is conditioned on membership in an officially recognized national race. This is the source of the problem, not merely a failure of implementation.

The National Unity Government's Federal Democracy Charter explicitly recognizes Rohingya rights. However, this recognition must be translated into concrete legal commitments.

Any future political transition in Myanmar must make the repeal of the 1982 Citizenship Law and its replacement with a citizenship system based on residence, birth, and descent a foundational requirement. Such a framework must comply with international human rights standards and cannot be treated as an aspirational goal to be addressed only after other political priorities.

In the immediate term:

  1. AThe NUG should formally adopt and publicly release a citizenship roadmap for the Rohingya. This roadmap should specify the legal reforms it would implement during a political transition and demonstrate that its human rights commitments extend to the population most severely excluded under the current system.
  2. BInternational donors and development partners that condition support on governance reforms should explicitly identify the repeal of the 1982 Citizenship Law as a measurable benchmark.
  3. CThe ICJ genocide proceedings should be supported with comprehensive documentation demonstrating how statelessness functioned as a legal precondition for the atrocities committed in 2017. This would help establish, as a matter of international law, that legislated statelessness can serve as a preparatory mechanism for genocide.

2. Establish a Regional Documentation and Birth Registration Framework

Across all three countries, the primary administrative barrier to citizenship is the lack of documentation.

Many stateless individuals cannot provide birth records, family lineage documents, or other evidence required by citizenship application procedures. This is not necessarily because they lack a legitimate claim to citizenship. Rather, documentation systems were never designed to reach their communities.

Hill tribe villages in northern Thailand, remote coastal communities in Sabah, and displaced Rohingya families all share a common problem. Their long-term presence has never been officially recorded in a form that citizenship authorities can easily process.

ASEAN, in partnership with UNHCR and the ISI, should develop a regional mobile documentation framework that brings registration services directly to affected communities rather than requiring residents to navigate distant and complex central bureaucracies.

Specific measures should include the following:

  1. AThailand's October 2024 Cabinet Resolution should be evaluated regularly based on measurable citizenship outcomes. Dedicated funding should support mobile registration units operating in Chiang Mai, Chiang Rai, and Mae Hong Son provinces, where stateless populations are concentrated.
  2. BMalaysia should establish a dedicated Sabah Documentation Taskforce responsible for registering the citizenship claims of long-term indigenous and cross-border communities within a clearly defined period. Civil society organizations should participate in monitoring the process.
  3. CMyanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia should commit to universal birth registration. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Global competition for critical minerals, including nickel, cobalt, gold, lithium, and rare earth elements, is reshaping international trade and diplomacy in ways that existing frameworks were not designed to manage.

Two distinct developments are converging. In Indonesia, the world's largest nickel producer, a ban on exports of unprocessed ore has redirected hundreds of billions of dollars in manufacturing investment into the country. At the same time, it has triggered a landmark World Trade Organization (WTO) dispute with the European Union (EU) that remains practically unresolved years after it began.

Across the African Sahel, military governments in Burkina Faso and Mali have nationalized mines, expelled Western operators, and made resource sovereignty a central pillar of their post-coup political identities. Russia has filled much of the security and commercial vacuum left by departing Western partners.

In the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), M23 rebels backed by Rwanda captured Goma and Bukavu in early 2025. Their advances gave them control over mineral-rich eastern provinces where competition over cobalt, coltan, and tantalum has financed armed groups for decades.

These cases differ substantially in political character. Indonesia is a democratic state pursuing industrial development through domestic resource-processing policies. Burkina Faso and Mali are governed by military regimes that use mineral revenues to finance counterinsurgency operations and consolidate political power. The DRC's crisis lies at the intersection of armed conflict, great-power competition, and resource extraction.

What all three cases share is that resource-producing states and political actors now exercise greater leverage over mineral supplies than they did a decade ago. The international frameworks governing trade, investment, and mineral supply chains were not designed for this reality.

This brief argues for an approach that takes the development claims of producing states seriously while establishing governance conditions under which mineral revenues benefit populations rather than political or military elites. It does not treat resource nationalism as a single problem that can be addressed through one policy response. Instead, it examines three overlapping phenomena that require differentiated tools.

I. WHY RAW MATERIALS BECAME A GEOPOLITICAL BATTLEGROUND

For most of the post-Cold War era, international trade in raw materials operated according to a broadly liberal model. Producing countries exported unprocessed commodities, while consuming countries added value through refining and manufacturing and retained most of the resulting economic returns.

This division of labor was not politically neutral. It reflected the greater negotiating power of consuming countries when international trade rules were established, as well as the relative political and economic weakness of many resource-producing states.

Three developments have disrupted this arrangement.

First, the clean energy transition has generated rapidly rising and geographically concentrated demand for minerals such as nickel, cobalt, lithium, manganese, and rare earth elements. These resources are located primarily in a relatively small number of countries. In 2022, the International Energy Agency (IEA) projected that demand for critical minerals could increase by between two and six times by 2040, depending on the pace of global decarbonization.

Second, China has moved strategically to dominate not only mineral extraction but also processing and manufacturing across critical mineral supply chains. It has become the world's leading refiner of cobalt, lithium, and rare earth elements, as well as one of the largest upstream mining investors in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America.

Third, worsening relations between the United States and China have transformed access to minerals into a national security concern for Western governments. Efforts to diversify supply chains have increased the perceived strategic importance of producing countries almost overnight.

According to a 2026 analysis by the global law firm Gibson Dunn, "resource nationalism has moved well beyond royalty disputes." It now includes export bans, quota systems, domestic processing mandates, and extraterritorial technology controls.

Verisk Maplecroft's Resource Nationalism Index for the first quarter of 2025 found that 47 developing countries, including 17 major producers of critical minerals, had experienced a significant increase in government control over mining and energy sectors since 2020.

Producing states are no longer simply increasing royalty rates. They are restricting exports, requiring domestic processing, renegotiating contracts during their terms, and, in the case of Mali, detaining foreign corporate executives.

The specific tools differ, but the underlying logic is consistent. Producing states want to retain a larger share of the value generated by their natural resources, and they now possess greater leverage to demand it than at any point in recent decades.

II. INDONESIA: THE NICKEL EXPORT BAN AND THE WTO'S LIMITS

Indonesia possesses the world's largest nickel reserves. In January 2020, the government of President Joko Widodo introduced a complete ban on exports of unprocessed nickel ore. The policy was designed to force mineral processing to occur domestically and to attract battery and stainless steel manufacturing investments that had previously gone to China, South Korea, and Europe.

According to the Indonesian government, annual revenues from nickel exports increased from approximately US$1 billion before the ban to at least US$34 billion afterward. Widodo repeatedly cited this increase as evidence that retaining and processing the resource domestically was the correct policy.

The EU challenged the measure at the WTO in November 2019, before the ban had even taken effect.

The EU's legal argument was straightforward. Indonesia's export prohibition violated Article XI:1 of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) 1994, which generally prohibits WTO members from restricting exports through measures other than duties, taxes, or other charges.

On November 30, 2022, a WTO panel agreed with the EU. It found that Indonesia's export ban and related domestic processing requirements were inconsistent with its WTO obligations and were not justified under any applicable exception. The panel upheld all of the EU's claims.

Indonesia's response fundamentally changed the dispute. Rather than comply with the ruling or negotiate a settlement, Indonesia filed an appeal on December 8, 2022. However, it appealed to the WTO Appellate Body, which has been unable to operate since 2019 because the United States has blocked the appointment of new members.

The appeal therefore could not proceed. This maneuver, which the EU later described as an "appeal into the void," suspended the binding resolution of the dispute indefinitely while allowing Indonesia to continue implementing its policy.

Recognizing that the standard WTO dispute settlement process had become ineffective, the EU launched consultations in July 2023 on the potential use of its Enforcement Regulation. This mechanism permits the EU to adopt countermeasures when WTO dispute settlement procedures are blocked.

The EU also proposed resolving the appeal through the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA), an alternative mechanism established by willing WTO members during the Appellate Body's absence. Indonesia has shown little interest in pursuing either option.

As of mid-2026, the dispute remains formally open but practically frozen. Thirty-one WTO disputes are now reportedly caught in similar void-appeal situations.

The EU's legal position is strong, but its political position is more complicated.

For years, Indonesia watched as raw nickel was extracted, exported to China, refined, and returned in the form of manufactured products that sold for many times the value of the original ore.

The export ban produced the industrialization that it was intended to generate. Foreign companies constructed processing plants, smelters, and battery facilities in Indonesia instead of locating them elsewhere. This investment created employment and industrial capacity that might otherwise not have developed.

The EU is effectively challenging a middle-income country for pursuing industrialization through domestic resource-processing requirements. European states used comparable policy tools during their own periods of industrial development.

Whether the EU's position is sustainable as a matter of long-term trade diplomacy is a question that the WTO dispute itself cannot resolve.

Indonesia has also become a model for other producing states. Vietnam has strengthened state control over rare earth mining and prohibited exports of unprocessed materials. The DRC introduced a cobalt export ban in February 2025 and later replaced it with production quotas, disrupting battery manufacturers around the world. Chile maintains a state-led approach to lithium development.

The Indonesia case therefore represents a broader challenge to the existing trade system. WTO restrictions on export controls are likely to face repeated challenges from developing countries seeking to industrialize through domestic mineral processing. At the same time, the enforcement mechanism for those rules has been allowed to collapse.

III. THE SAHEL: RESOURCE SOVEREIGNTY UNDER MILITARY GOVERNANCE

Burkina Faso and Mali represent a fundamentally different dimension of the broader shift toward resource sovereignty. In these countries, mineral policy cannot be separated from military governance, the deliberate dismantling of Western partnerships, and a strategic realignment toward Russia.

Burkina Faso experienced two military coups in 2022. Captain Ibrahim Traoré, who seized power in September of that year, has made control over mineral resources central to his political legitimacy.

Gold accounts for more than 70 percent of Burkina Faso's export revenues. In 2023, the military government revised the country's mining code to increase state royalties and established the Société de Participation Minière du Burkina (SOPAMIB) as a vehicle for direct state participation in the mining sector.

In August 2024, the government nationalized the Boungou and Wahgnion mines for approximately US$80 million. This was significantly below the US$300 million valuation previously attached to the sale of the assets.

In June 2025, the government transferred five additional mines and exploration permits from Endeavour Mining and Lilium to SOPAMIB.

In April 2025, the junta awarded an industrial mining license for the Niou gold deposit to the Russian company Nordgold. Nordgold's subsidiary holds an 85 percent stake in the project, which is expected to produce more than 20 tonnes of gold over an eight-year period.

By the end of 2025, Burkina Faso's total gold production had reached a record 94 tonnes, an increase from approximately 60 tonnes in 2024. This growth resulted from both industrial expansion and the formalization of artisanal mining output that had previously remained outside official reporting systems.

The security architecture surrounding these policies is inseparable from their economic dimensions.

Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger are all governed by military regimes and have expelled French military forces. The three governments formed the Alliance of Sahel States and formally withdrew from the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) in January 2025.

Russia has moved into much of the political and security space previously occupied by France. It provides military cooperation through the Africa Corps, the successor to the Wagner Group, while receiving mining access and diplomatic alignment in return.

Mali's trajectory is more advanced and provides a clearer indication of what this model may produce over time.

Under Colonel Assimi Goita, who has extended military rule until 2027, Mali has taken aggressive action against Western mining companies.

In November 2024, Resolute Mining's chief executive, Terence Holohan, and two colleagues were detained in Bamako over alleged tax irregularities. The company agreed to pay the Malian government US$160 million, including an initial payment of US$80 million followed by later installments, in order to secure their release.

Barrick Gold subsequently had four employees detained in a separate dispute. The Malian government claimed that Barrick owed US$500 million in unpaid taxes, an allegation that the company disputed.

This pattern includes the detention of executives, coercive settlements, and contract renegotiations under a mining code that allows the state to acquire stakes of up to 35 percent in mining projects.

These actions have unsettled the West African gold sector and raised serious questions about whether foreign investment in states governed by military regimes can be meaningfully separated from operations conducted under legal and political coercion.

IV. THE DRC: MINERAL WEALTH AND CONFLICT GOVERNANCE

The DRC presents a third form of resource politics, situated at the intersection of mineral competition and active armed conflict.

In early 2025, the Rwanda-backed M23 rebel group gained effective control over large portions of the country's eastern provinces. M23 captured Goma, the capital of North Kivu Province, in late January and Bukavu, the capital of South Kivu Province, in February.

Both cities are important centers of regional mineral trade.

Competition over cobalt, coltan, tin, tungsten, and tantalum has long contributed to the conflict. In May 2024, M23 seized Rubaya, a mining area estimated to account for approximately 15 percent of global tantalum production. The capture demonstrated the group's ability to use mineral revenues to strengthen its military and political position.

At the national level, mining has accounted for approximately 46 percent of the DRC's gross domestic product in recent years. The country contains mineral reserves that are essential to the clean energy transition, including approximately 70 percent of the world's cobalt supply.

Mining operations in the eastern provinces have attracted substantial global investment. At the same time, artisanal and industrial mineral production has helped finance armed groups that have made effective governance nearly impossible.

Two major diplomatic processes related to the conflict unfolded in 2025.

On June 27, 2025, the DRC and Rwanda signed a ceasefire agreement in Washington under US mediation.

On December 4, 2025, the parties signed a broader set of agreements at a presidential ceremony in Washington. These included a US-DRC Strategic Partnership Agreement focused on mineral access and a US-Rwanda Framework for Shared Economic Prosperity.

Supporters praised the agreements as a potential framework for peace. Critics argued that they primarily served as mechanisms for securing US commercial access to Congolese minerals.

M23 continued operating in contested mineral areas after both signing ceremonies. In January 2026, Congolese plaintiffs filed legal challenges against the December agreement on constitutional grounds.

The EU suspended cooperation with Rwanda in early 2025 in response to evidence of mineral smuggling from conflict areas.

A lawsuit filed by the DRC government against Apple, alleging that the company had sourced minerals supplied through networks connected to M23, remained pending as of mid-2026.

These disputes illustrate the difficulty of separating supply chain accountability from geopolitical interests. Under both EU and US frameworks, governments seek to regulate conflict minerals while simultaneously treating access to the DRC's mineral reserves as a strategic priority. Civilian protection consequently risks becoming secondary to commercial and geopolitical objectives.

V. WHAT CONNECTS THESE CASES

Indonesia's export ban, Burkina Faso's mine nationalizations, and the DRC's conflict mineral crisis appear very different. Treating all three simply as examples of "resource nationalism" obscures more than it explains.

Indonesia's policy is an industrial strategy implemented by a functioning democracy. Burkina Faso's policies involve revenue extraction by a military government aligned with Moscow. The DRC's crisis centers on conflict financing within a broader security collapse and an international competition for access to resources.

A policy designed for one of these situations will fail if applied to the others.

What connects the cases is not their political character, but the global economic structure that produced them.

For most of the postwar period, the value generated through mineral extraction moved upward through global supply chains. Refiners, manufacturers, and consumers in wealthier countries captured much of the value, while producing countries retained relatively little.

This arrangement continued as long as producing states lacked the leverage to demand a greater share.

The clean energy transition changed this balance. Demand increased sharply for minerals located in a relatively small number of countries, giving producing states and local political actors greater negotiating power.

The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development's World Investment Report 2025 documents this shift in the extractive industries. Producing states are increasingly adopting measures that investors consider unfavorable, including foreign equity restrictions, domestic processing requirements, and more stringent permit conditions.

These policies reflect dissatisfaction with an economic model that enabled resource extraction without producing comparable levels of domestic development.

The measures may take the form of a democratically enacted export prohibition, a military decree nationalizing a mine, or an armed group seizing control of a processing facility. Although their political legitimacy differs, they all respond to the same underlying reality: the traditional global division of labor no longer appears acceptable to many countries and communities that possess the resources.

Consuming states have been slow to acknowledge this shift. Doing so would require them to reconsider trade rules that were largely written to their advantage.

The consequences of this delay are visible in the unresolved Indonesia dispute, the collapse of Western influence in the Sahel, and the persistent failure to distinguish legitimate mineral governance reforms from the conflict financing that makes the DRC's resources both strategically valuable and politically dangerous.

VI. POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

The following five recommendations are directed toward the EU, the United States, ASEAN, ECOWAS, and relevant multilateral institutions.

They are intended to recognize the legitimate development interests of mineral-producing states while addressing the governance failures that allow mineral revenues to finance repression, conflict, and political consolidation rather than public welfare.

The recommendations reflect the distinctions established in Section V. Indonesia requires negotiation and a revised international legal framework. Burkina Faso and Mali require conditional engagement and stronger requirements for revenue transparency. In the DRC, traceability and civilian protection must become preconditions for investment agreements rather than secondary objectives.

1. Reform WTO Rules to Recognize Processing Requirements as Development Tools

The EU's legal position in its dispute with Indonesia is formally correct under current WTO rules. However, it is likely to become politically unsustainable over the long term.

Taking legal action against a middle-income country for attempting to industrialize may be defensible under existing trade law, but it undermines the EU's credibility as a development partner.

This is especially true because the EU's Critical Raw Materials Act contains domestic content provisions that may raise comparable WTO concerns. Similarly, the US Inflation Reduction Act under the Biden administration imposed local content requirements without facing an equivalent WTO challenge.

WTO members should begin negotiations to expand the development exceptions available under GATT Article XVIII. The revised framework should establish a time-limited and transparency-based exception for domestic mineral-processing requirements.

Producing countries below a defined per capita income threshold should be permitted to adopt temporary processing mandates for critical minerals. These policies should be subject to UNCTAD review to determine whether they are producing genuine downstream industrialization.

Participating states should publish annual data on processing investments, employment creation, and domestic value addition.

Consuming countries that impose similar domestic content requirements within their own industries should not challenge comparable policies in developing countries without also subjecting their own measures to equivalent scrutiny.

2. Restore the Functioning of the WTO Appellate Body

Indonesia's ability to maintain a void appeal, together with the 30 other disputes reportedly in the same position, is a direct consequence of the United States blocking appointments to the WTO Appellate Body since 2017.

This dysfunction does not protect the rules-based trading system. It benefits whichever party finds noncompliance convenient.

Indonesia used this institutional gap strategically, and other governments have followed its example.

The EU, Canada, Japan, and Australia should make restoration of the Appellate Body a formal priority in their bilateral trade and technology discussions with the United States.

Expanding participation in the MPIA would also reduce the ability of nonparticipating states to avoid binding dispute resolution.

WTO members should additionally establish a graduated countermeasure protocol that permits proportionate retaliation in cases involving void appeals without requiring governments to activate full enforcement mechanisms.

Such a protocol would provide an intermediate option between passive acceptance and a broader trade confrontation. It would also create a stronger deterrent against the type of procedural strategy that Indonesia has helped normalize.

3. Link Development Finance to Mineral Revenue Transparency

The central problem with resource nationalism under military rule is not simply that governments assert sovereignty over natural resources.

The deeper problem is that mineral revenues may flow into military budgets, off-budget accounts, and payments to foreign security contractors rather than healthcare, education, infrastructure, and other public services.

Western governments and mining companies that continue operating in military-governed states without transparency requirements may effectively help finance authoritarian consolidation.

Multilateral development banks, including the World Bank's International Finance Corporation and the African Development Bank, should establish a Critical Minerals Governance Compact.

Under this framework, producing countries would commit to publishing mineral revenues, contract terms, and beneficial ownership information in exchange for preferential access to development finance, technical assistance for industrial policy, and favorable market access.

Participation in the EU's Global Gateway initiative and the US Minerals Security Partnership should be conditioned on adherence to the Compact and compliance with civilian governance benchmarks.

For countries transitioning from military to civilian government, multilateral institutions should also consider establishing a Mineral Revenue Escrow mechanism.

Under such a system, a portion of mining royalties would be held in a multilateral account during the transition period. The funds would be released for public services following a verified transfer to civilian government.

This mechanism could create a financial incentive for political transition that economic isolation alone has failed to provide.

4. Distinguish Legitimate Governance Reform from Coercive Extraction in the Sahel

The situations in Burkina Faso and Mali require Western governments to draw a distinction that they have often failed to make.

A producing state has a legitimate interest in capturing a larger share of the value generated by its mineral resources. However, this interest is different from the use of resource revenues to finance military rule, suppress civilian populations, or coerce foreign companies.

Conflating these issues leads to one of two ineffective responses.

The first is complete disengagement, which allows Russian companies and security actors to expand their influence without improving conditions for local populations.

The second is uncritical engagement, which treats greater state revenue collection as equivalent to genuine development regardless of how the funds are used.

Investment agreements with democratic producing states should offer preferential market access for processed minerals in exchange for supply chain transparency and safeguards against excessive dependence on a single foreign joint-venture partner.

For states governed by military regimes, continued engagement should depend on measurable requirements. These should include publication of mining revenue allocations, prohibitions against using mineral revenues to finance operations targeting civilians, and demonstrated progress toward civilian governance.

The withdrawal of the Alliance of Sahel States from ECOWAS does not require complete Western disengagement. It requires a recalibrated approach that uses remaining points of leverage, including regional transportation systems, financial arrangements, and currency relationships, without legitimizing military rule.

5. Make Civilian Protection a Precondition for Access to DRC Minerals

The December 2025 Washington agreements between the DRC, Rwanda, and the United States represent a meaningful diplomatic effort. Peace through investment can be a legitimate strategy when investment produces measurable improvements in security and governance.

The current structure, however, appears to have secured mineral access before achieving civilian protection.

Independent analysts and Congolese civil society organizations have documented that M23 continued operating in mineral-producing regions after both the June and December signing ceremonies.

Congolese lawyers and human rights advocates filed constitutional challenges against the Strategic Partnership Agreement in January 2026.

The United States and the EU should therefore make civilian protection benchmarks a precondition for activating mineral access provisions under the Washington agreements.

These conditions should include the verified withdrawal of M23 forces from artisanal mining areas and the confirmed cessation of mineral smuggling through Rwanda before exclusive investment rights take effect.

Governments should also enforce traceability and due diligence requirements under existing supply chain accountability laws for minerals transported through Rwanda, regardless of broader diplomatic relations.

Approximately 200,000 artisanal miners depend on cobalt and coltan mining areas in eastern DRC for their livelihoods.

Any formalization process that removes these miners without providing viable alternative sources of income will deepen local grievances and may contribute to future conflict.

VII. CONCLUSION

The clean energy transition transformed critical minerals into strategic resources with remarkable speed. Producing states recognized the resulting shift in leverage before consuming countries and international institutions adjusted.

Indonesia understood that global competition for nickel gave it bargaining power that it had not previously possessed. It used that leverage deliberately, even after receiving an unfavorable WTO ruling that it could avoid implementing through a procedural appeal.

Military governments in the Sahel recognized that international demand for gold and other resources reduced the economic consequences of breaking with France and ECOWAS. They built their post-coup political identities partly around this protection.

The DRC's extraordinary mineral wealth attracted investments that might otherwise have gone elsewhere. It also intensified a conflict that has made effective governance increasingly difficult.

These outcomes reflect decisions made by producing governments, consuming states, and international institutions that adapted too slowly to a world in which critical minerals had become strategic assets rather than ordinary commodity inputs.

The dispute involving Indonesia will eventually be resolved through bilateral negotiations, WTO reform, countermeasures, or the gradual erosion of legal certainty produced by the current void-appeal system.

The Sahel's relationship with Western mining companies is being reconstructed on new terms, assuming that it is reconstructed at all.

The DRC's mineral governance crisis will not be resolved through investment agreements that treat peace as necessary for commercial activity while failing to treat civilian protection as an equally binding obligation.

All three cases require a more honest assessment of who benefited from the previous global mineral system and who did not.

They also require governments and international institutions to develop rules that reflect current economic and political realities rather than continuing to enforce arrangements that no longer command the support of the countries they are intended to govern.

The consequences of further delay are already visible.

In Indonesia, the result is a trade system in which participation in dispute resolution has become effectively optional.

In the Sahel, Western engagement has been replaced by Russian security contractors and state-controlled mining partnerships.

In the DRC, armed groups are financed through minerals that may eventually enter the batteries of electric vehicles.

These outcomes are not acceptable. They are predictable consequences of international frameworks designed for a world that no longer exists.
